Waiting times, gamma losses, and the normal model

6.1 Positive severities and unrestricted measurements

A count model describes how many claims occur. A severity model describes the size of one loss. What features should a severity distribution preserve? Positive support rules out negative losses; a scale parameter sets the monetary size; a shape parameter can alter skewness. Waiting times raise another question: does surviving an initial period change the remaining waiting-time law? Exponential, gamma, and normal distributions provide distinct answers.

All examples in this chapter are stipulated mathematical models, not empirical descriptions of an insurer. A density family supplies consequences conditional on its assumptions. Assessing those assumptions requires observations and a separate statistical analysis. In particular, a model for a positive claim severity does not automatically include the probability of no claim during an exposure.

6.2 The exponential law and residual waiting time

For rate \(\beta\gt0\), an exponential variable \(T\) has density \(f_T(t)=\beta e^{-\beta t}\), \(t\gt0\), and zero elsewhere. The rate has reciprocal units: if \(T\) is measured in years, \(\beta\) is measured per year. Integrating the density gives \(F_T(t)=1-e^{-\beta t}\) for \(t\ge0\), so the survival function is \(\overline F_T(t)=P(T\gt t)=e^{-\beta t}\).

The tail-area formula gives \(E[T]=\int_0^\infty e^{-\beta t}dt=1/\beta\). Integration by parts gives the second moment: \(E[T^2]=\int_0^\infty t^2\beta e^{-\beta t}dt=2\int_0^\infty te^{-\beta t}dt=2/\beta^2\). The endpoint term vanishes because exponential decay dominates the polynomial. Subtracting the squared mean gives variance \(1/\beta^2\).

For \(s,t\ge0\), the event \(T\gt s+t\) lies inside \(T\gt s\), so conditional probability yields

\[P(T-s\gt t\mid T\gt s)=\frac{e^{-\beta(s+t)}}{e^{-\beta s}}=e^{-\beta t}=P(T\gt t).\]

This is the memoryless property. Under this model, knowing that a waiting time already exceeds \(s\) does not change the distribution of its remaining duration. For \(\beta=0.5\), the expected total waiting time is two time units. After surviving two units, the probability of waiting more than three additional units is \(e^{-1.5}\), approximately 0.223130. Memorylessness concerns conditional residual duration, not an assertion that physical systems cannot age.

For a positive differentiable survival function, the hazard rate is \(h(t)=f_T(t)/\overline F_T(t)\). It is the limiting conditional event probability per unit interval after survival to \(t\). Indeed \(P(t\lt T\le t+\Delta\mid T\gt t)=[F_T(t+\Delta)-F_T(t)]/\overline F_T(t)\), and dividing by \(\Delta\) then taking the limit gives the ratio. Exponential hazard equals the constant \(\beta\). A time-varying hazard violates this exponential assumption.

6.3 A shape parameter through the gamma function

The exponential family fixes the ratio of standard deviation to mean at one. To vary that relative dispersion while retaining positive support, define the gamma function by \(\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty z^{a-1}e^{-z}dz\), \(a\gt0\). Near zero the power is integrable because \(a\gt0\); at infinity exponential decay makes the integral finite. Integration by parts gives \(\Gamma(a+1)=a\Gamma(a)\). Since \(\Gamma(1)=1\), positive integers satisfy \(\Gamma(m)=(m-1)!\).

A gamma variable \(X\) with shape \(\alpha\gt0\) and rate \(\beta\gt0\) has density

\[f_X(x)=\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x},\qquad x\gt0.\]

The substitution \(z=\beta x\) transforms its integral into \(\Gamma(\alpha)/\Gamma(\alpha)=1\). Shape is dimensionless and rate has reciprocal units. Another convention uses scale \(\theta=1/\beta\); silently swapping these conventions changes every moment. This book always gives gamma parameters as shape and rate.

For a real exponent \(r\) with \(\alpha+r\gt0\), the same substitution yields \(E[X^r]=\beta^{-r}\Gamma(\alpha+r)/\Gamma(\alpha)\). At \(r=1,2\), the gamma recurrence gives

\[E[X]=\frac\alpha\beta,\qquad E[X^2]=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\beta^2},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac\alpha{\beta^2}.\]

Consequently the coefficient of variation, defined as standard deviation divided by positive mean, is \(1/\sqrt\alpha\). Increasing shape while holding the mean fixed requires setting \(\beta=\alpha/E[X]\); it reduces relative dispersion. Shape one reproduces the exponential law exactly. For \(\alpha=3,\beta=0.5\), the mean is six and variance twelve, while a mean-six exponential would have variance thirty-six.

For \(\alpha\gt1\), differentiate the logarithm of the density: \((\log f_X)'=(\alpha-1)/x-\beta\). Its unique zero is \((\alpha-1)/\beta\), where the derivative changes from positive to negative, giving the density mode. For \(\alpha=1\), the density decreases from its boundary value; for \(0\lt\alpha\lt1\), it diverges near zero but remains integrable. A density singularity at zero does not create an atom there: the probability of a single point remains zero.

Noninteger gamma shapes are legitimate because normalization uses an integral defined for every positive real shape. The parameter need not count a literal number of physical stages. The density and its moments already provide an operational positive-loss model. A waiting-time sum interpretation needs an additional joint model; Chapter 10 derives the two-stage sum after establishing the required convolution formula.

6.4 The normal law and standardization

A standard normal variable \(Z\) has density \(\phi(z)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-z^2/2}\) on the whole real line. The normalization constant comes from a two-dimensional integral. If \(I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^2/2}dz\), then nonnegative integration and polar coordinates give \(I^2=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}r\,dr\,d\vartheta=2\pi\). Since \(I\gt0\), it equals \(\sqrt{2\pi}\).

Symmetry makes \(E[Z]=0\): the integrand \(z\phi(z)\) is odd and absolutely integrable. Since \(\phi'(z)=-z\phi(z)\), integration by parts gives \(E[Z^2]=\int z^2\phi(z)dz=-\int z\phi'(z)dz=\int\phi(z)dz=1\). The boundary term \(z\phi(z)\) vanishes at both infinities. Thus the density has the claimed mean zero and variance one.

Define \(X=\mu+\sigma Z\), where \(\mu\in\mathbb R\) and \(\sigma\gt0\). This is the normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Linearity and variance scaling prove these moments. Writing \(\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)\), monotonicity of the transformation gives

\[P(a\lt X\le b)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right).\]

The standardized variable \((X-\mu)/\sigma\) is dimensionless. Symmetry gives \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\). The CDF has no elementary antiderivative, so software or numerical tables evaluate these probabilities. The standard library’s complementary error function supplies \(1-\Phi(z)=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(z/\sqrt2)\), where \(\operatorname{erfc}(v)=\tfrac2{\sqrt\pi}\int_v^\infty e^{-u^2}du\), as the substitution \(z=\sqrt2u\) verifies.

Normal support includes negative numbers. A normal variable with mean six and standard deviation two assigns probability \(\Phi(-3)\), approximately 0.00134990, to negative values. For a nonnegative gross loss, this is a model mismatch even when numerically small. Truncating the negative part or setting it to zero changes the law and its moments. Later aggregate approximations may use a normal law with an explicit approximation boundary; this chapter treats the normal family itself exactly.

6.5 Read parameters through their consequences

The original program verifies exponential residual survival, gamma moments under the rate convention, and a normal negative-support probability. Every output is a consequence of the stipulated parameters. A computation cannot validate the family choice for observed losses, but it can expose a rate/scale error or quantify a support mismatch before the model is used.

"""Exact family formulas, with floating-point consistency checks."""
from math import erfc, exp, isclose, sqrt

rate = 0.5
s, t = 2.0, 3.0
conditional = exp(-rate*(s + t))/exp(-rate*s)
assert isclose(conditional, exp(-rate*t))
alpha, beta = 3.0, 0.5
mean, variance = alpha/beta, alpha/beta**2
assert (mean, variance) == (6.0, 12.0)
normal_negative = 0.5*erfc(3/sqrt(2))
assert 0.0013 < normal_negative < 0.0014
print(f"Exponential residual survival at 3 = {conditional:.6f}")
print(f"Gamma(shape=3, rate=0.5): mean {mean:.1f}; variance {variance:.1f}")
print(f"Normal(mean=6, sd=2): P(X < 0) = {normal_negative:.8f}")

Exponential residual survival at 3 = 0.223130
Gamma(shape=3, rate=0.5): mean 6.0; variance 12.0
Normal(mean=6, sd=2): P(X < 0) = 0.00134990

You can now distinguish a constant-hazard waiting model, a flexible positive severity family, and a location-scale law on the real line. Each family carries assumptions that persist into every subsequent calculation.

6.6 Compare families at the same financial scale

Matching two moments makes a useful comparison, but it does not make two laws equal. A mean-six, variance-twelve gamma law has shape three and rate one-half. A normal law with the same moments has mean six and standard deviation \(\sqrt{12}\). The gamma assigns no probability to negative loss. The normal assigns probability \(\Phi(-\sqrt3)\), approximately 0.0416, to negative values. That mismatch is larger than in the earlier standard-deviation-two illustration, because the relative dispersion is now larger.

Even at positive thresholds, the tails differ. Integrating the integer-shape-three gamma density twice by parts gives survival \(e^{-\beta t}[1+\beta t+(\beta t)^2/2]\), \(t\ge0\). At threshold twelve and rate one-half, the survival is \(25e^{-6}\), approximately 0.06197. The moment-matched normal gives \(1-\Phi(\sqrt3)\), approximately 0.04163. These are exact family formulas with rounded numerical evaluations; no asymptotic approximation identifies one tail with the other.

The familiar normal interval percentages are specific to a normal law. Symmetry gives \(P(|Z|\le a)=2\Phi(a)-1\). At \(a=1,2,3\), numerical integration gives approximately 0.682689, 0.954500, and 0.997300. Three standard deviations still leave positive tail probability, approximately 0.002700. Merely standardizing an arbitrary variable to mean zero and variance one does not give these probabilities: a symmetric sign variable has mean zero, variance one, and all its mass inside the closed interval \([-1,1]\).

The memoryless property is also restrictive enough to characterize its family under suitable hypotheses. Suppose \(T\ge0\), its survival \(G(t)\) is positive for every finite \(t\), \(G(0)=1\), and it satisfies \(G(s+t)=G(s)G(t)\). Let \(a=-\log G(1)\). Integer subdivision gives \(G(m/n)=e^{-am/n}\) for nonnegative rational \(m/n\). Monotonicity and right continuity extend this equality to every real \(t\ge0\). Properness, \(G(t)\to0\), requires \(a\gt0\), yielding an exponential law. The hypotheses exclude an immortal lifetime with \(G\equiv1\) and the separate degenerate-at-zero possibility.

6.7 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. An exponential waiting time has mean four years. Find its rate and probability of exceeding two years.

An exponential waiting time has mean four years. Its rate is \(1/4\) per year, not four per year. Its probability of exceeding two years is \(e^{-2/4}=e^{-1/2}\). Calling four a scale and one-quarter a rate keeps the units consistent.

Exercise 2. Find gamma shape and rate for mean ten and variance twenty-five.

Find gamma shape and rate for mean ten and variance twenty-five. The equations \(\alpha/\beta=10\) and \(\alpha/\beta^2=25\) give \(\beta=10/25=0.4\) and \(\alpha=4\). Substitution checks both moments; scale would be 2.5.

Exercise 3. Derive the gamma inverse moment E[1/X] and identify when it is finite.

For \(\alpha\gt1\), use the moment formula with \(r=-1\) to obtain \(E[1/X]=\beta\Gamma(\alpha-1)/\Gamma(\alpha)=\beta/(\alpha-1)\). For \(\alpha\le1\), the integral diverges at zero. Positive mean does not guarantee a finite inverse moment.

Exercise 4. For a normal variable with mean 100 and standard deviation 15, express P(85<X<=130) through the standard normal CDF.

For normal \(X\) with mean 100 and standard deviation 15, express \(P(85\lt X\le130)\). Standardization gives lower threshold \(-1\) and upper threshold 2, hence \(\Phi(2)-\Phi(-1)\). Replacing 15 by variance 225 in the denominator would be dimensionally and mathematically incorrect.

Exercise 5. Derive the shape-two gamma survival function and show that this law is not memoryless.

A shape-two rate-\(\beta\) gamma law has survival \(e^{-\beta t}(1+\beta t)\), obtained by integrating \(\beta^2xe^{-\beta x}\) from \(t\) to infinity. Its residual survival is \(e^{-\beta t}[1+\beta(s+t)]/(1+\beta s)\), which generally differs from \(e^{-\beta t}(1+\beta t)\). The exponential special case does not extend to every gamma shape.
