Concentration, tail bounds, and the law of large numbers

A probability approximation estimates a tail. A concentration inequality bounds it. The difference matters whenever an error must be controlled rather than merely expected to be small.

Original supplement. The official syllabus lists inequalities and tail bounds in Week 8, but no Week 8 recordings appear in the supplied playlist. This appendix is independently authored material. It does not reconstruct or claim to summarize those missing lectures. The proofs below are supplied here; the law-of-large-numbers context can also be studied in Grinstead and Snell, Introduction to Probability, Chapter 8.

A nonnegative variable controls its own tail

Suppose an insurer observes a nonnegative random loss \(X\), measured in currency units, with finite expectation \(\mu=\mathbb E[X]\). Fix a threshold \(a>0\) in the same units. The question is how much probability can lie above \(a\) if only the mean is known. On the event \(\{X\ge a\}\), the loss is at least \(a\). Outside that event, the loss remains nonnegative. Consequently the pointwise inequality

\[X\ge a\,\mathbf1_{\{X\ge a\}}\]

holds for every outcome. Here \(\mathbf1_A\) is the indicator of an event \(A\): it equals one on \(A\) and zero elsewhere. Taking expectations preserves the inequality, because the expectation of a nonnegative difference is nonnegative. Since \(\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A]=\Pr(A)\), division by \(a\) gives Markov's inequality:

\[\Pr(X\ge a)\le\frac{\mu}{a}.\]

This is an exact upper bound under the stated assumptions. If the ratio exceeds one, replace it by one. A negative-valued variable does not satisfy the pointwise comparison: a large positive tail can be offset by a large negative part in the expectation. Nonnegativity is therefore a structural hypothesis.

The inequality can be attained. Let \(X=a\) with probability \(p\), and let \(X=0\) otherwise, for \(0\le p\le1\). Then \(\mu=ap\) and \(\Pr(X\ge a)=p=\mu/a\). Without further information about the distribution, a uniformly smaller bound is impossible. A loose bound for one loss family can still be optimal over the entire class specified only by nonnegativity and mean.

Variance turns a mean into an error bound

Let \(X\) now be any real-valued random variable with finite mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2=\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2]\). To bound deviations from the mean, apply Markov's inequality to the nonnegative variable \((X-\mu)^2\). For a tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), the events \(\{|X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon\}\) and \(\{(X-\mu)^2\ge\varepsilon^2\}\) are equal. Hence

\[\Pr(|X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}.\]

This is Chebyshev's inequality. The square is essential: it both produces a nonnegative variable and makes the expectation equal the variance. The tolerance has the units of the loss, so the ratio is dimensionless. The result controls both tails together and holds for discrete, continuous, and mixed distributions.

A one-sided event such as \(\{X-\mu\ge\varepsilon\}\) is a subset of the two-sided event, so the same bound also applies to it. That does not make the bound a one-sided equality. A distribution concentrated near its mean can have a much smaller tail; a rare extreme value can use much of the allowed variance.

The weak law from the variance of an average

Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent and identically distributed losses, each with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\). Their sample average is \(\overline X_n=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\). Linearity of expectation gives \(\mathbb E[\overline X_n]=\mu\). Independence makes all distinct-pair covariances zero, so

\[\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.\]

Applying Chebyshev to the average gives the finite-sample statement

\[\Pr(|\overline X_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\min\!\left(1,\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\right).\]

For each fixed positive tolerance, the right side tends to zero as \(n\) tends to infinity. This proves the weak law of large numbers in the independent, identically distributed, finite-variance setting: \(\overline X_n\) converges to \(\mu\) in probability. Convergence in probability means precisely that the probability of an error larger than any fixed positive tolerance tends to zero. It does not say that every finite sample lies close to the mean.

The argument actually needs only common means, common finite variances, and pairwise zero covariances. Independence is a convenient sufficient condition. Under common shocks, the covariance terms need not vanish. For example, if every \(X_i\) equals the same random variable \(Z\), then \(\overline X_n=Z\) for every \(n\); increasing the portfolio size produces no diversification. The number of contracts alone cannot establish an averaging benefit.

The central limit theorem answers a different question. It describes a rescaled distribution and can supply an approximate tail probability. The weak law establishes concentration around the mean. Neither result turns an assumed distribution or an independence assumption into an observed property of a real insurance portfolio.

Exponential bounds use information about the whole distribution

Suppose the moment generating function \(M_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]\) is finite at some \(t>0\). The function \(e^{tX}\) is nonnegative and increasing in \(X\). Applying Markov to it yields

\[\Pr(X\ge a)=\Pr(e^{tX}\ge e^{ta})\le e^{-ta}M_X(t).\]

Every admissible positive value of \(t\) gives a bound. Taking the infimum over such values can improve it. This family of exponential bounds is often called the Chernoff method. The free parameter is mathematical; it is chosen to minimize a proved upper bound, not fitted until a simulation looks favorable.

For an explicit example, assume independent losses \(X_i\) have an exponential distribution with rate \(\beta>0\). Its mean is \(1/\beta\), and direct integration gives \(M_X(t)=\beta/(\beta-t)\) for \(t<\beta\). For an average threshold \(a>1/\beta\), independence gives

\[\Pr(\overline X_n\ge a)\le\left[e^{-ta}\frac{\beta}{\beta-t}\right]^n,\qquad0<t<\beta.\]

Minimize the logarithm, \(n[-ta-\log(1-t/\beta)]\). Its derivative is \(n[-a+1/(\beta-t)]\); setting this to zero gives \(t_*=\beta-1/a\). This value is positive because the threshold exceeds the mean, and it is smaller than \(\beta\). The second derivative \(n/(\beta-t)^2\) is positive, so this stationary point is the minimum. Substitution gives

\[\Pr(\overline X_n\ge a)\le\exp\{-n[\beta a-1-\log(\beta a)]\}.\]

For \(r>1\), the function \(r-1-\log r\) is positive: it vanishes at one and has derivative \(1-1/r>0\). The bound therefore decays exponentially with \(n\) at any fixed threshold above the mean. That stronger rate uses the exponential distribution assumption, which Chebyshev does not need.

If a severity has no finite positive moment generating function, this proof supplies no positive-tail exponential bound. A lognormal severity is a familiar example: a finite mean and variance do not ensure a finite positive MGF. Chebyshev remains available whenever the variance exists. The choice of inequality follows the information and assumptions, not a hierarchy in which one method always wins.

A reproducible comparison

The following original program compares bounds for twenty independent exponential losses with mean 2,000 currency units and an average-loss threshold of 3,000. The first bound uses only nonnegativity and the mean; the second uses finite variance and independence; the third also uses the exponential MGF. The program evaluates proved formulas. It does not estimate the actual probability by simulation.

"""Exact algebraic bounds for an exponential sample mean; no simulation."""
from math import exp, log

mean = 2000.0
n = 20
threshold = 3000.0
delta = threshold - mean
markov = mean / threshold
chebyshev = mean ** 2 / (n * delta ** 2)
ratio = threshold / mean
chernoff = exp(-n * (ratio - 1 - log(ratio)))
assert 0 < chernoff < chebyshev < markov < 1
print(f'Markov upper-tail bound:    {markov:.6f}')
print(f'Chebyshev two-tail bound:   {chebyshev:.6f}')
print(f'Chernoff upper-tail bound:  {chernoff:.6f}')
for sample_size in [20, 100, 1000]:
    bound = min(1.0, mean ** 2 / (sample_size * delta ** 2))
    print(f'n={sample_size:4d}: Chebyshev bound {bound:.6f}')

Markov upper-tail bound:    0.666667
Chebyshev two-tail bound:   0.200000
Chernoff upper-tail bound:  0.150966
n=  20: Chebyshev bound 0.200000
n= 100: Chebyshev bound 0.040000
n=1000: Chebyshev bound 0.004000

When deciding how many observations are sufficient, the Chebyshev formula can be solved in the other direction. To make the displayed error probability at most \(\delta>0\), a sufficient integer sample size is \(n\ge\max(1,\lceil\sigma^2/(\delta\varepsilon^2)\rceil)\). This is a conservative design criterion under the specified model, not a minimum achievable sample size.

Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Prove Markov's inequality when the event uses a strict inequality.

Pointwise, \(X\ge a\mathbf1_{\{X>a\}}\) still holds. Take expectations and divide by \(a>0\) to obtain \(\Pr(X>a)\le\mathbb E[X]/a\). Equality need not be attained by the two-point example because the mass at exactly \(a\) is excluded. The same upper bound is valid, while the equality example depends on the event convention.

Exercise 2. An iid loss has variance \(v\). Derive a sufficient sample size for an average error at least \(e>0\) to have probability at most \(q\in(0,1)\).

The average variance is \(v/n\). Chebyshev bounds the error probability by \(v/(ne^2)\). Requiring this to be at most \(q\) gives \(n\ge v/(qe^2)\). The smallest integer meeting this sufficient condition is \(\lceil v/(qe^2)\rceil\), with at least one observation required. The argument supplies a sufficient condition and makes no assertion that fewer samples fail.

Exercise 3. Every pair of distinct portfolio losses has covariance \(c\), and every individual variance is \(v\). What happens to the average variance?

Expand the variance of the sum before dividing by \(n^2\). There are \(n\) variance terms and \(n(n-1)\) ordered covariance terms. Thus \(\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)=v/n+(n-1)c/n\). For a family with fixed positive \(c\), this tends to \(c\), so the preceding Chebyshev proof cannot establish concentration at the mean. A covariance prescription must itself define valid joint laws; arbitrary negative fixed \(c\) cannot persist for every \(n\).

Exercise 4. Derive the Chernoff upper-tail bound for \(N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)\), with \(\lambda>0\), at a threshold \(a>\lambda\).

The MGF is \(\exp(\lambda(e^t-1))\), so the logarithm of the bound is \(-ta+\lambda(e^t-1)\). Its derivative vanishes at \(t_* =\log(a/\lambda)>0\); its second derivative is positive. Substitution gives \(\Pr(N\ge a)\le\exp[-a\log(a/\lambda)+a-\lambda]\). For integer-valued \(N\), a noninteger threshold can first be replaced by its ceiling to sharpen the bound. The result remains an upper bound rather than an exact Poisson tail.

Exercise 5. Explain why a CLT approximation cannot replace the Chebyshev bound in a proof of a fixed finite-sample error guarantee.

A CLT states convergence of distribution functions after centering and scaling as \(n\) tends to infinity. By itself it supplies no numerical remainder at the finite \(n\) under study. Substituting a normal tail therefore produces an approximation. Chebyshev is an inequality valid at every finite \(n\) satisfying its moment assumptions. A finite-sample normal-approximation guarantee would require an additional error theorem and its additional hypotheses.
