Losses, payments, and deductibles

17.1 What does the insurer actually pay?

A loss of 800 does not imply an insurance payment of 800. A deductible, a coverage exclusion, or a payment limit can change the amount. Pricing therefore begins by specifying a function of the physical loss. A fitted distribution for losses is only an input to that calculation. This chapter constructs the payment distribution for an ordinary deductible and explains why the average payment in a claims database differs from the average cost per loss.

Let \(X\geq0\) denote the gross monetary loss from one loss event during a one-year contract. Its cumulative distribution function is \(F_X(x)=P(X\leq x)\), and its survival function is \(\overline F_X(x)=P(X>x)\). We permit a continuous distribution, a discrete distribution, or a mixture of both. A policy's ordinary deductible \(d\geq0\) is the amount the policyholder absorbs before the insurer pays the excess. With no payment limit, the insurer's payment per loss is

\[Y=(X-d)_+=\max(X-d,0).\]

Thus a loss of 800 with a deductible of 200 generates a payment of 600. A loss of 150 generates zero. All monetary quantities use the same stipulated currency unit. Here “per loss” includes those losses generating no payment. It does not include years in which no loss occurs; annual occurrence belongs in a separate frequency model.

The retained loss is \(R=X-Y=\min(X,d)\). The identity \(X=R+Y\) holds outcome by outcome. Consequently \(E[X]=E[R]+E[Y]\) whenever the expectations are finite. The corresponding variances do not generally add: retained loss and payment depend on the same \(X\). This observation is useful whenever a contract partitions a financial exposure into components.

17.2 A continuous loss can produce an atom at zero

For \(y\geq0\), the event \(Y\leq y\) is exactly the event \(X\leq d+y\). For negative \(y\), it is impossible. Hence

\[F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}0,&y<0,\\F_X(d+y),&y\geq0.\end{cases}\]

In particular, \(P(Y=0)=F_X(d)\). An atom is a point with positive probability. Even when \(X\) has a density, \(Y\) usually has an atom because every loss in an interval maps to zero. On the positive half-line, a continuous loss with density \(f_X\) gives the payment density \(f_Y(y)=f_X(d+y)\). Its integral is \(P(X>d)\), rather than one. The remaining probability is at zero.

Original worked example. Suppose \(X\) is uniform on \((0,1000)\) and \(d=200\). Then \(P(Y=0)=0.2\), and the positive density is \(1/1000\) on \((0,800)\). Its area is 0.8. The mean is \(\int_0^{800}y/1000\,dy=320\). Modeling \(Y\) as uniform on \((0,800)\) would instead give 400, because it silently removes the zero payments.

When the gross-loss law has atoms, transformation by events remains valid without a density. If \(P(X=200)=0.1\), that probability belongs at \(Y=0\) when \(d=200\). An atom at \(X=500\) becomes an atom at \(Y=300\). Event algebra is more general than a change-of-variable density formula.

17.3 Payment per loss and payment given a payment

Let \(q=P(X>d)\), and assume \(q>0\). Define \(Y^{P}\) to have the conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(Y>0\). This is the per-payment distribution. It describes a positive payment selected from the population of paid claims. It is not a variable that takes zero when no payment occurs; that role belongs to \(Y\).

\[P(Y^{P}\leq y)=\frac{F_X(d+y)-F_X(d)}q,\qquad y\geq0.\]

The numerator counts gross losses between \(d\) and \(d+y\); division by \(q\) renormalizes after selecting losses above the deductible. For every positive integer \(k\) for which the moment exists,

\[E[Y^k]=qE[(Y^{P})^k].\]

To prove this, split the expectation between \(Y=0\) and \(Y>0\). The first part vanishes because \(0^k=0\); conditional expectation gives the second. Writing \(m_P=E[Y^P]\) and \(v_P=\operatorname{Var}(Y^P)\), we obtain

\[E[Y]=qm_P,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y)=qv_P+q(1-q)m_P^2.\]

The second term measures the additional variability of switching payment on or off. It follows by substituting \(E[Y^2]=q(v_P+m_P^2)\) into \(E[Y^2]-E[Y]^2\). In the uniform example, \(Y^P\) is uniform on \((0,800)\), so its mean is 400. Multiplication by 0.8 recovers the per-loss mean of 320.

A database containing only positive payments estimates \(m_P\), not \(E[Y]\). To estimate expected cost per loss, one also needs \(q\), which cannot generally be inferred from paid claims alone. To estimate annual cost, one further needs the number of gross-loss events or the number of positive payments, with the corresponding severity basis. Removing zeros changes both the count and the severity distribution.

17.4 Tail integrals derive deductible costs

For each fixed nonnegative number \(y\), the identity \(y=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{\{y>t\}}dt\) expresses length as accumulated indicators. Applying it to a nonnegative random variable and interchanging nonnegative integration and expectation gives

\[E[Y]=\int_0^\infty P(Y>t)\,dt=\int_d^\infty\overline F_X(x)\,dx.\]

The interchange is justified by Tonelli's theorem: nonnegative terms can be integrated in either order, even if the answer is infinite. More generally, integrating \(ky^{k-1}\) from zero to \(y\) gives

\[E[Y^k]=k\int_0^\infty t^{k-1}\overline F_X(d+t)\,dt.\]

This formula works for atoms and densities alike. For a continuous loss, direct integration \(\int_d^\infty(x-d)^kf_X(x)dx\) is equivalent. The tail form exposes how changing the deductible removes a strip of survival probability. If \(\pi(d)=E[(X-d)_+]\) is finite, then

\[\pi(d+h)-\pi(d)=-\int_d^{d+h}\overline F_X(x)dx\quad(h>0).\]

At continuity points of the survival function, \(\pi'(d)=-\overline F_X(d)\). A one-unit increase in deductible reduces expected payment approximately by the probability of a positive payment. Because survival decreases, the slope becomes less negative as the deductible increases. Thus \(\pi\) is decreasing and convex. At atoms, use one-sided derivatives instead of silently assuming differentiability.

17.5 Memorylessness is a special severity property

Original worked example. Let \(X\) be exponential with mean \(\theta=500\), so \(\overline F_X(x)=e^{-x/500}\) for \(x\geq0\). For deductible \(d=250\), \(q=e^{-1/2}\). The positive residual payment has survival

\[P(Y^P>y)=\frac{P(X>d+y)}{P(X>d)}=e^{-y/500}.\]

Thus the per-payment mean remains 500 even though the deductible reduces the per-loss mean. This is exponential memorylessness, derived here from a survival ratio. The first two per-loss raw moments are \(500q\) and \(2(500)^2q\). Consequently the variance is \(500^2(2q-q^2)\). A fixed deductible reduces how often the insurer pays, while this particular severity model leaves the conditional payment law unchanged.

This property is not typical. For a uniform gross loss, raising the deductible decreases the upper endpoint of the positive residual. For a heavy-tailed law with \(\overline F_X(x)=(1+x/b)^{-a}\), where \(a,b>0\), conditioning gives \(P(Y^P>y)=(1+y/(b+d))^{-a}\). If \(a>1\), its mean is \((b+d)/(a-1)\), which increases with the deductible. Selecting more severe losses can outweigh subtracting a larger fixed amount.

An ordinary deductible must also be distinguished from a franchise deductible, whose payment is \(X\mathbf1_{\{X>d\}}\). Under a franchise provision, crossing the threshold activates payment of the entire loss. For \(X=800,d=200\), it pays 800 rather than 600. For any gross-loss law, the expected franchise payment exceeds the expected ordinary payment by \(dP(X>d)\). This follows by subtracting the two payment functions outcome by outcome.

17.6 Mean excess separates selection from retention

The conditional mean residual \(e(d)=E[X-d\mid X>d]\), when defined and finite, is called the mean excess function. It answers how much additional loss remains once the deductible has been crossed. The per-loss expected payment is \(\pi(d)=\overline F_X(d)e(d)\). Thus a deductible can lower expected insurer cost even while the average positive payment rises.

Suppose gross loss has a differentiable density and positive survival near \(d\). Define its hazard rate by \(h(d)=f_X(d)/\overline F_X(d)\). In a small interval above \(d\), the conditional probability of loss falling in that interval is approximately hazard times interval width. Differentiating the ratio \(e(d)=\pi(d)/\overline F_X(d)\), using \(\pi'(d)=-\overline F_X(d)\), gives

\[e'(d)=h(d)e(d)-1.\]

The two terms distinguish stronger selection from subtracting another unit of deductible. Exponential loss has constant hazard \(1/\theta\) and constant mean excess \(\theta\), so the derivative vanishes. Uniform loss on \((0,b)\) has \(e(d)=(b-d)/2\) for \(0\leq d<b\), so the derivative is −1/2. Both results agree with the conditional payment laws derived earlier.

For the survival \((1+x/b)^{-a}\) with \(a>1\), hazard is \(a/(b+d)\) and mean excess is \((b+d)/(a-1)\). Their product minus one is \(1/(a-1)\), the positive derivative of mean excess. Larger paid claims after a deductible increase can therefore arise solely from selection under an unchanged gross-loss distribution. It need not indicate inflation or deterioration of underlying risk.

These comparisons also warn against transporting exponential intuition to every insurance line. A stable average paid claim under changed deductibles is a model-specific prediction, not a universal actuarial identity. The conditional distribution or its survival ratio should be checked before assuming memorylessness.

17.7 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. A discrete deductible law. Losses are 100, 400, and 1000 with probabilities 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2. Find the per-loss and per-payment first two moments for d=250.

The payment values are 0, 150, and 750. Thus \(q=0.5\), \(E[Y]=45+150=195\), and \(E[Y^2]=6750+112500=119250\). The variance is \(119250-195^2=81225\). Conditional positive probabilities are 0.6 and 0.4. The conditional mean is 390, second moment 238500, and variance \(238500-390^2=86400\). Multiplying the conditional raw moments by 0.5 recovers the per-loss raw moments; multiplying the conditional variance alone would not.

Exercise 2. Deductible sensitivity. For exponential mean θ, choose d to halve the expected payment per loss, and determine the positive-payment mean.

The tail integral gives \(\pi(d)=\theta e^{-d/\theta}\). Setting this equal to \(\theta/2\) gives \(d=\theta\log2\). The probability of payment is then one-half. The survival ratio is \(e^{-y/\theta}\), so the conditional mean is still \(\theta\). Halving expected cost did not halve the average paid claim.

Exercise 3. Finite mean but infinite variance. Let the survival function be (1+x/b) to power −a, with 1<a≤2. Does an ordinary deductible make the payment variance finite?

The mean is \(\int_d^\infty(1+x/b)^{-a}dx=b(1+d/b)^{1-a}/(a-1)\), which is finite. The second moment is \(2\int_0^\infty t(1+(d+t)/b)^{-a}dt\). Its integrand has the order of \(t^{1-a}\) at infinity, whose integral diverges for \(a\leq2\). Hence variance remains infinite. Removing small losses cannot remove a heavy upper tail.

Exercise 4. Retention dependence. For X uniform on (0,2) and d=1, compute Cov(min(X,1),(X−1)+).

The retained mean is \(\int_0^1x/2\,dx+1/2=3/4\). The payment mean is \(\int_1^2(x-1)/2\,dx=1/4\). Their product is zero below one and \(X-1\) above one, so its expectation is 1/4. The covariance is \(1/4-3/16=1/16\). This explicit positive covariance explains why adding retained and insurer variances alone underestimates gross-loss variance.

Exercise 5. Identify a contract from its payment function. Compare ordinary and franchise deductibles for X uniform on (0,1000), d=200. Give expected payments and explain their difference.

The ordinary expected payment is 320. The franchise mean is \(\int_{200}^{1000}x/1000\,dx=480\). Their difference is 160, equal to \(200\times0.8\). Both contracts pay with probability 0.8, but each positive franchise payment includes the otherwise retained deductible. Equal payment frequencies do not imply equal severity distributions.

17.8 Reproducible payment calculation

The original program enumerates a discrete loss model, computes conditional moments by renormalization, and checks the variance decomposition. It calculates finite examples exactly with rational arithmetic. Such checks detect implementation errors; they do not replace the distributional arguments above.

"""Original exact discrete deductible calculation, using fractions."""
from fractions import Fraction as F

losses = [(100, F(1, 2)), (400, F(3, 10)), (1000, F(1, 5))]
deductible = 250
payments = [(max(x - deductible, 0), p) for x, p in losses]
q = sum(p for y, p in payments if y > 0)
mean = sum(y * p for y, p in payments)
second = sum(y**2 * p for y, p in payments)
variance = second - mean**2
positive_mean = sum(y * p / q for y, p in payments if y > 0)
positive_second = sum(y**2 * p / q for y, p in payments if y > 0)
positive_variance = positive_second - positive_mean**2
assert mean == q * positive_mean == 195
assert second == q * positive_second == 119250
assert variance == q * positive_variance + q * (1 - q) * positive_mean**2
assert variance == 81225
print(f"Payment probability: {q}")
print(f"Per-loss mean, second moment, variance: {mean}, {second}, {variance}")
print(f"Per-payment mean, variance: {positive_mean}, {positive_variance}")

Payment probability: 1/2
Per-loss mean, second moment, variance: 195, 119250, 81225
Per-payment mean, variance: 390, 86400

17.9 Sources and perspective

Related lecture material: W10 L1, 19:44: per-loss and per-payment language; W10 L2, 15:58: exponential deductible calculation. The payment-basis distinction is also discussed in the community-authored Loss Data Analytics, §3.4.1, Policy Deductibles. The contract function is the mathematical specification; proposed administrative or behavioral motivations do not establish its effect on actual policyholder behavior. The present model holds the gross-loss law fixed when varying a deductible. If the contract changes behavior or reporting, that change requires a new gross-loss or observation model, rather than a different integration rule. Our examples and derivations are original. The reader can now transform a gross-loss law into a mixed payment law, recover conditional positive-payment moments, and identify which frequency basis an annual pricing calculation requires.
