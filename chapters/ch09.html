9.1 A loss changes when its payment rule changes

A distribution for gross loss does not immediately give the distribution of an insurer's payment. An ordinary deductible, a payment cap, an inflation multiplier, or a nonlinear measurement rule replaces a loss \(X\) by a function \(Y=g(X)\). The task is to determine the entire law of \(Y\), including any point masses. Substitution into a density without checking the shape of \(g\) can omit probability or put it on an impossible region.

Let \(X\) be a real random variable with cumulative distribution function \(F_X(x)=P(X\leq x)\). Let \(g\) be a measurable real function; measurability ensures that \(Y=g(X)\) is a random variable. Its distribution function is always

\[F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\leq y).\]

This statement is the cumulative-distribution-function method. Its strength is that it works before we know whether \(Y\) is discrete, continuous, or mixed. The essential step is solving the inequality in the original variable. A derivative formula can then shorten the calculation when the transformation satisfies additional assumptions.

9.2 Increasing and decreasing transformations

Assume first that \(X\) has a density \(f_X\) on an interval \(I\), and that \(g\) is strictly increasing and differentiable there, with nonzero derivative. Its inverse \(h=g^{-1}\) maps the transformed interval \(g(I)\) back to \(I\). For \(y\) inside that transformed interval, \(g(X)\leq y\) is equivalent to \(X\leq h(y)\). Differentiating \(F_Y(y)=F_X(h(y))\) yields \(f_Y(y)=f_X(h(y))h'(y)\). Outside the transformed support, the density is zero.

If \(g\) is strictly decreasing, the inequality reverses: \(g(X)\leq y\) means \(X\geq h(y)\). Continuity of the law of \(X\) gives \(F_Y(y)=1-F_X(h(y))\), and differentiation gives \(-f_X(h(y))h'(y)\). Since \(h'\) is negative, both cases combine into

\[f_Y(y)=f_X(h(y))|h'(y)|,\qquad y\in g(I).\]

The absolute value makes probability nonnegative. The derivative belongs to the inverse transformation, not the forward map. This is the one-dimensional version of preserving probability under a change of coordinates: a small output interval of width \(dy\) corresponds to an input interval of width approximately \(|h'(y)|dy\).

For an original inflation example, let \(X\) be exponential with rate \(\beta>0\), meaning \(f_X(x)=\beta e^{-\beta x}\) for \(x>0\). If \(Y=aX\) with \(a>0\), then \(h(y)=y/a\), and \(f_Y(y)=(\beta/a)e^{-\beta y/a}\) for \(y>0\). The rate decreases by a factor \(a\), while the mean increases from \(1/\beta\) to \(a/\beta\). The inverse derivative supplies the normalizing factor that a bare substitution would miss.

A decreasing example exposes the sign issue. If \(X\) is uniform on \((0,1)\) and \(Y=1-X\), then \(h'(y)=-1\); the density remains one on \((0,1)\). Without the absolute value the alleged density would be negative. For \(Y=1/X\) on the same input support, \(h(y)=1/y\), so \(f_Y(y)=1/y^2\) for \(y>1\). Integrating this density gives one, but \(E[Y]=\int_1^\infty y^{-1}\,dy\) diverges. A bounded input can produce an unbounded variable with infinite mean if the function is singular near an endpoint.

9.3 Several inputs can lead to one output

Exponential transformations connect light-tailed variables to important positive-loss families. If \(Z\) is normal with mean \(\mu\) and positive standard deviation \(\sigma\), then \(X=e^Z\) has support \((0,\infty)\). Taking logarithms gives \(F_X(x)=\Phi((\log x-\mu)/\sigma)\) for \(x>0\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF. Differentiation yields \(f_X(x)=\exp[-(\log x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)]/(x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})\). This defines the lognormal law. The factor \(1/x\) is the inverse derivative; omitting it destroys normalization.

If instead \(Z\) is exponential with rate \(\beta\) and \(X=e^Z\), then \(X>1\) and its density is \(\beta e^{-\beta\log x}/x=\beta x^{-\beta-1}\). Its survival is \(x^{-\beta}\) for \(x\geq1\), a Pareto law with lower endpoint one. For positive \(r\), \(E[X^r]=\int_1^\infty\beta x^{r-\beta-1}dx\), finite exactly when \(r<\beta\), in which case it equals \(\beta/(\beta-r)\). The same exponential map therefore produces distinct positive families from normal and exponential inputs, with sharply different moment behavior.

The square transformation is not one-to-one across the real line. For \(Y=X^2\), the event \(Y\leq y\) is empty when \(y<0\); for \(y\geq0\), it is \(-\sqrt y\leq X\leq\sqrt y\). Assume here that \(X\) has a density \(f_X\), so its CDF has no jumps. Then

\[F_Y(y)=F_X(\sqrt y)-F_X(-\sqrt y),\qquad y\geq0.\]

Differentiating at regular points gives the density identity \(f_Y(y)=[f_X(\sqrt y)+f_X(-\sqrt y)]/(2\sqrt y)\) for almost every \(y>0\). Both inverse branches contribute. If one branch lies outside the support, its density contribution is zero. The single point \(y=0\) does not need a finite density value, because changing a density at one point changes no probability.

For \(X\) uniform on \((-1,1)\), each branch has input density \(1/2\). Thus \(f_Y(y)=1/(2\sqrt y)\) on \((0,1)\), and \(F_Y(y)=\sqrt y\) there. The density diverges near zero, yet \(\int_0^1(2\sqrt y)^{-1}dy=1\). An unbounded density is permissible; an infinite atom at zero is not implied. In fact \(P(Y=0)=P(X=0)=0\).

More generally, split the input support into disjoint intervals on which \(g\) is differentiable and one-to-one. Let \(h_j\) be the inverse on branch \(j\), with nonzero derivative at regular output values. Probabilities from disjoint input branches add, producing \(f_Y(y)=\sum_j f_X(h_j(y))|h_j'(y)|\), summing only branches whose inverse belongs to the input support. This formula needs the stated branch conditions; flat intervals require separate treatment. The CDF method remains the safer starting point when critical values or atoms complicate the branch picture.

9.4 Flat payment rules create atoms

Let \(X\geq0\) be continuous and let \(d>0\) be an ordinary deductible. The payment per loss is \(Y=(X-d)_+=\max(X-d,0)\). Every loss at most \(d\) maps to zero, so \(P(Y=0)=F_X(d)\). For \(y\geq0\), solving the inequality gives \(F_Y(y)=F_X(d+y)\). Hence the positive part has density \(f_X(d+y)\) for \(y>0\). Its integral is \(1-F_X(d)\), not one, because the remaining probability sits at zero.

If \(X\) is exponential with rate \(\beta\), the zero mass is \(1-e^{-\beta d}\), and the positive density is \(\beta e^{-\beta(d+y)}\). Conditional on receiving a positive payment, divide this density by \(P(Y>0)=e^{-\beta d}\). The per-payment density is then \(\beta e^{-\beta y}\), which integrates to one. The per-loss and per-payment distributions answer different questions and have different means: \(e^{-\beta d}/\beta\) versus \(1/\beta\).

A cap produces an atom at the other end. If \(Y=\min(X,u)\) with \(u>0\), then \(P(Y=u)=P(X\geq u)=1-F_X(u)\) for continuous \(X\). The density below \(u\) remains \(f_X(y)\); there is no density formula that represents the cap mass as an ordinary finite function. Its CDF jumps at \(u\), and that jump is precisely the point mass. With both deductible and cap, \(Y=\min((X-d)_+,u)\) has atoms at zero and \(u\), plus density \(f_X(d+y)\) on \((0,u)\).

For a discrete input, the mechanism is even more direct: collect all preimages. The probability \(P(Y=y)\) equals \(\sum_{x:g(x)=y}P(X=x)\). If \(X\) takes \(-2,-1,1,2\) with probabilities \(0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4\), then \(X^2\) equals one with probability \(0.5\) and four with probability \(0.5\). A derivative formula would be irrelevant because the input law has no density.

9.5 Choosing a method and checking the result

A useful calculation begins with the range of \(g\), because this determines the output support. Next solve \(g(X)\leq y\), keeping track of reversed inequalities and multiple branches. Locate flat regions and input atoms before differentiating. The final law must have nonnegative mass and density whose combined total is one.

Moments provide a second check that does not require deriving a transformed density. For integrable \(g(X)\), \(E[Y]=E[g(X)]\); with a density this is \(\int g(x)f_X(x)dx\). In the uniform-square example, the transformed-law calculation gives \(E[Y]=\int_0^1 y/(2\sqrt y)dy=1/3\). Directly, \(E[X^2]=(1/2)\int_{-1}^1x^2dx=1/3\). Agreement checks the normalization, branches, and Jacobian in this example.

An inverse-CDF construction reverses the same logic and produces random variables with a desired law. For a CDF \(F\), define its generalized inverse \(Q(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\geq u\}\) for \(0<u<1\). If \(U\) is uniform on \((0,1)\), then \(Q(U)\) has CDF \(F\). The key equivalence is \(Q(u)\leq x\) exactly when \(u\leq F(x)\), apart from endpoint conventions that do not affect the uniform law. Hence \(P(Q(U)\leq x)=P(U\leq F(x))=F(x)\). Unlike an ordinary inverse, the generalized inverse remains meaningful when a CDF has jumps or flat intervals.

For an exponential law with rate \(\beta\), solve \(u=1-e^{-\beta x}\) to obtain \(Q(u)=-\log(1-u)/\beta\). This gives an exact distributional simulation construction from a uniform source. For a payment with zero mass \(q\) and positive exponential component of rate \(\beta\), take \(Q(u)=0\) for \(u\leq q\), and \(Q(u)=-\log[(1-u)/(1-q)]/\beta\) for \(u>q\). The interval of uniform inputs mapping to zero has length \(q\), so the atom is represented by the construction rather than approximated by a narrow density peak.

The same reasoning explains quantiles after strictly increasing transformations. If \(g\) is continuous and strictly increasing and the relevant quantile is uniquely determined, the pth quantile of \(g(X)\) is \(g(Q_X(p))\). It does not imply that the mean transforms in this way. For \(X\) uniform on \((0,1)\), \(E[X^2]=1/3\), whereas \(E[X]^2=1/4\). Quantiles refer to ordered probability thresholds; means average numerical magnitudes.

A transformation changes the random quantity, not the probability of an underlying event. Keeping that distinction explicit prevents the common mistake of applying the density formula on an unchanged support. It also explains why the CDF method survives when a smooth density description fails. The insurer's payment law can now be derived from the contract rule without discarding zero payments or capped claims.

9.6 Exercises with worked solutions

For every transformed law, state the support before writing the density. A final normalization check should include every point mass. If a map has two inverse branches, test the range over which each branch belongs to the original support. The exercises use these checks to distinguish a valid distribution from an algebraically plausible expression.

Exercise 1. Let X be uniform on (0,2). Find the law and mean of Y=3X+1.

The inverse is \((y-1)/3\), and the support is \((1,7)\). Therefore \(f_Y(y)=(1/2)(1/3)=1/6\) there, zero elsewhere. Its mean is \((1+7)/2=4\), agreeing with \(3E[X]+1=4\).

Exercise 2. Let X have density 2x on (0,1). Find the law of Y=X².

There is one positive branch. For \(0<y<1\), \(F_Y(y)=P(X\leq\sqrt y)=\int_0^{\sqrt y}2x\,dx=y\). Thus \(Y\) is uniform on \((0,1)\). The density formula gives \(2\sqrt y/(2\sqrt y)=1\), confirming it.

Exercise 3. Let X be uniform on (−2,1). Find the density of X² with its correct pieces.

Input density is \(1/3\). Both roots are available when \(0<y<1\), giving \(1/(3\sqrt y)\). Only the negative root remains when \(1<y<4\), giving \(1/(6\sqrt y)\). The integrals are \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), totaling one. Values at the piece boundaries do not affect the law.

Exercise 4. For exponential X with rate 1/2, deductible 2, and payment cap 4, state every component of the per-loss payment law.

For \(Y=\min((X-2)_+,4)\), the zero mass is \(1-e^{-1}\). The density is \((1/2)e^{-(2+y)/2}\) for \(0<y<4\). The cap mass is \(P(X\geq6)=e^{-3}\). The continuous mass is \(e^{-1}-e^{-3}\), and all three components sum to one.

Exercise 5. Let X be uniform on (0,1). Derive the density of Z=−log X and identify its mean.

For \(z\geq0\), \(Z\leq z\) is equivalent to \(X\geq e^{-z}\), so \(F_Z(z)=1-e^{-z}\). Differentiation gives \(f_Z(z)=e^{-z}\) on \((0,\infty)\), an exponential law with rate one and mean one. Direct integration \(\int_0^1-\log x\,dx=1\) supplies the same mean.

9.7 Checking branches and masses numerically

The program integrates the two pieces of the asymmetric square density and checks an ordinary-deductible cap law. Midpoint quadrature is applied after a square-root substitution to remove the integrable endpoint singularity.

"""Check transformed supports, inverse branches, and payment atoms."""
from math import exp, isclose


def midpoint(function, left, right, count=10000):
    width = (right-left)/count
    return width*sum(function(left+(i+.5)*width) for i in range(count))


# Substitute y=u^2, dy=2u du in the square-density pieces.
branch_mass = midpoint(lambda u: 2/3, 0, 1)
branch_mass += midpoint(lambda u: 1/3, 1, 2)
mean_square = midpoint(lambda u: 2*u*u/3, 0, 1)
mean_square += midpoint(lambda u: u*u/3, 1, 2)
assert isclose(branch_mass, 1, abs_tol=1e-10)
assert isclose(mean_square, 1, abs_tol=1e-8)
zero_mass = 1-exp(-1)
cap_mass = exp(-3)
continuous_mass = exp(-1)-exp(-3)
assert isclose(zero_mass+cap_mass+continuous_mass, 1)
print(f"asymmetric square density mass = {branch_mass:.8f}")
print(f"asymmetric square mean = {mean_square:.8f}")
print(f"payment atoms: zero = {zero_mass:.8f}, cap = {cap_mass:.8f}")
print(f"continuous payment mass = {continuous_mass:.8f}")
