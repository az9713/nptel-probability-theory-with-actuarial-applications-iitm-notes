15.1 What becomes normal when many losses are added?

A portfolio contains many individually skewed losses. To estimate the probability that their sum exceeds available funds, we need more than its expected value. The central limit theorem explains why a standardized sum can approach a normal law. Its hypotheses and scaling matter: it does not claim that individual losses become normal, or that every large portfolio has an accurately normal extreme tail.

Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be independent and identically distributed real random variables with finite mean \(\mu\) and finite positive variance \(\sigma^2\). For a fixed positive integer \(n\), define the sum \(T_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\) and average \(\overline X_n=T_n/n\). Independence gives \(E[T_n]=n\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T_n)=n\sigma^2\). Standardization produces

\[Z_n=\frac{T_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}=\frac{\overline X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}.\]

The central limit theorem for this setting states that, for every real \(z\), \(P(Z_n\leq z)\to\Phi(z)\) as \(n\to\infty\), where \(\Phi\) is the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal variable. This is convergence in distribution. It controls fixed standardized thresholds asymptotically; it gives no numerical error tolerance for a particular \(n\) by itself.

The scale \(\sqrt n\) follows from variance. Dividing the centered sum by \(n\) makes its variance vanish, which describes concentration of the average. Dividing by \(\sqrt n\) leaves variance \(\sigma^2\), and dividing also by \(\sigma\) makes it one. The theorem identifies the limiting shape after this nondegenerate scaling.

15.2 Why the normal exponent appears

A short proof exposes the mechanism without assuming that a moment-generating function exists. Set \(W=(X_1-\mu)/\sigma\), so \(E[W]=0\) and \(E[W^2]=1\). Its characteristic function is \(\varphi_W(t)=E[e^{itW}]\), defined for every real \(t\), because the complex exponential has modulus one.

Finite second moment permits the local expansion \(\varphi_W(t)=1-t^2/2+o(t^2)\) as \(t\to0\). Here \(o(t^2)\) means a remainder whose ratio to \(t^2\) tends to zero. One justification expands the exponential through its quadratic term. The residual divided by \(t^2\) tends pointwise to zero and is bounded by a constant times \(W^2\); dominated convergence therefore allows expectation. The linear term vanishes because the mean is zero.

Independence now supplies the decisive product:

\[\varphi_{Z_n}(t)=\left[\varphi_W(t/\sqrt n)\right]^n=\left[1-\frac{t^2}{2n}+o(1/n)\right]^n\longrightarrow e^{-t^2/2}.\]

The limit is the characteristic function of the standard normal distribution. The characteristic-function continuity theorem converts this pointwise convergence into convergence in distribution. That theorem is the analytic bridge used here; its general proof is outside this chapter. The argument identifies the role of independence in multiplication and finite variance in the quadratic expansion.

If \(\sigma^2=0\), every \(X_i=\mu\) almost surely and standardization by \(\sigma\) is undefined. The sum is deterministic. If variance is infinite, the quadratic expansion need not hold. For example, independent Cauchy variables have characteristic function \(e^{-|t|}\); their average remains Cauchy because \([e^{-|t|/n}]^n=e^{-|t|}\). Averaging does not concentrate that distribution. This counterexample violates finite mean as well as finite variance, so it does not contradict the theorem.

15.3 Use the theorem at the correct threshold

Original worked example. Suppose 100 independent gross losses each have mean 200 and standard deviation 300. Let \(T_{100}\) be their total. The exact moment calculation gives mean 20000 and standard deviation \(300\sqrt{100}=3000\). A normal approximation gives

\[P(T_{100}>24500)\approx1-\Phi\left(\frac{24500-20000}{3000}\right)=1-\Phi(1.5)\approx0.0668.\]

The number is an approximation whose accuracy depends on the severity law. The first two moments alone do not certify the error. Nor does the theorem justify treating the sum as exactly normal: a nonnegative sum cannot be negative, whereas its normal approximation assigns some probability below zero.

A fixed monetary threshold corresponds to a standardized threshold that changes with \(n\). The basic theorem directly addresses fixed \(z\) in thresholds \(n\mu+z\sigma\sqrt n\). Far-tail questions, such as exceeding twice the mean for increasingly large \(n\), involve \(z\) growing with \(\sqrt n\). A normal approximation in that regime requires additional analysis and can misrepresent rare catastrophic events.

When \(\rho=E[|X_1-\mu|^3]<\infty\), a Berry–Esseen bound states that a universal finite constant \(C\) exists with \(\sup_z|P(Z_n\leq z)-\Phi(z)|\leq C\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)\). We invoke this quantitative theorem without proof or a chosen numerical constant. It explains why skewed or heavy-tailed models can require many more observations: the standardized absolute third moment matters, not just \(n\). Even a small absolute probability error can be large relative to a rare tail probability.

15.4 Continuity correction belongs to the event boundary

A continuous normal curve can approximate an integer-valued count by assigning each integer \(k\) the interval \([k-1/2,k+1/2]\). This is the continuity correction. It improves alignment of discrete event regions with continuous area, but it does not repair an otherwise unsuitable normal approximation.

Let an integer-valued variable \(B\) have mean \(m\) and standard deviation \(s>0\). If a normal approximation is appropriate, then

\[P(B\leq k)\approx\Phi((k+1/2-m)/s),\] \[P(B\geq k)\approx1-\Phi((k-1/2-m)/s).\]

For strict inequalities, first rewrite the integer event: \(B>k\) means \(B\geq k+1\), so its corrected lower boundary is \(k+1/2\). Similarly, \(B<k\) ends at \(k-1/2\). For integers \(a\leq b\), \(P(a\leq B\leq b)\) uses the interval from \(a-1/2\) to \(b+1/2\). The correction follows the included integers, not the direction of a memorized sign.

Original worked example. For \(B\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(100,0.1)\), mean is 10 and standard deviation is 3. To approximate \(P(B\geq15)\), use the boundary 14.5, yielding \(1-\Phi(1.5)\). Using 15.5 would approximate \(P(B\geq16)\), a different event. The program compares these approximations with the exact finite binomial sum.

For a monetary lattice with spacing \(h\), the half-width is \(h/2\), not universally 0.5 monetary units. If every claim pays 100 and the aggregate is \(100B\), the event \(100B\geq1500\) has a corrected monetary boundary of 1450. A sum of continuous losses needs no lattice correction.

15.5 Concentration and the law of large numbers

Original supplement. The supplied playlist has no Week 8 lesson labels. The following concentration argument supplies a logical bridge rather than reconstructing an absent recording. If \(V\geq0\) and \(a>0\), the pointwise inequality \(V\geq a\mathbf1_{\{V\geq a\}}\) gives Markov's inequality \(P(V\geq a)\leq E[V]/a\). Applying it to \((U-E[U])^2\) yields Chebyshev's inequality:

\[P(|U-E[U]|\geq a)\leq\operatorname{Var}(U)/a^2.\]

For the independent finite-variance average, this implies

\[P(|\overline X_n-\mu|\geq\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\longrightarrow0\qquad(\varepsilon>0).\]

This is a weak law of large numbers under the stated assumptions: the average converges in probability to its mean. It concerns concentration on the original scale, while the central limit theorem describes fluctuations on the shrinking \(1/\sqrt n\) scale. The weak-law proof needs only pairwise zero covariances and common finite variance; the classical central limit theorem stated above uses independent, identically distributed variables.

For mean 200 and standard deviation 300, Chebyshev gives \(P(|\overline X_{100}-200|\geq90)\leq1/9\). It is a finite-sample upper bound, often conservative. A normal calculation might be much smaller, but its accuracy is a separate question. A rigorous bound and a shape approximation answer different demands.

15.6 A sample size rule must depend on the distribution

The binomial count makes the limits of a universal sample size rule concrete. Let each summand be Bernoulli with success probability \(p\), where \(0<p<1\). Its centered third absolute moment is \(p(1-p)^3+(1-p)p^3\), because the two centered values are \(1-p\) and \(-p\). Dividing by variance to power three-halves gives

\[\frac{E[|X-p|^3]}{[p(1-p)]^{3/2}}=\frac{p^2+(1-p)^2}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}.\]

This quantity grows without bound as \(p\) approaches zero or one. Holding \(n\) fixed while making events rarer therefore worsens the generic normal error bound. Large trial count alone is insufficient. A million contracts with one-in-a-million event probability still have expected count one. Most years contain zero or one event, a strongly discrete pattern.

An approximation error must also be interpreted on the scale of the decision. An absolute error of 0.005 may be acceptable for a probability near one-half and unacceptable for a probability of 0.0001. The central limit theorem concerns convergence of distribution functions, rather than a universal bound on relative tail error. Continuity correction aligns integer cells but does not certify a remote tail.

Estimating \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) from observations introduces another issue. The theorem above treats them as true distribution parameters. Replacing \(\sigma\) by a sample standard deviation introduces another random variable. A studentized limit can often be derived under suitable conditions, but the substitution does not make a finite sample exactly normal. Parameter estimation and future-claim randomness should remain distinct in an exceedance calculation.

The binomial lab supplies a finite benchmark: the exact tail is about 0.07257 and the corrected normal result about 0.06681. Correction improves the uncorrected result here, yet leaves an error near 0.00577. The example demonstrates improvement rather than exactness. Reporting many decimal places does not establish matching approximation accuracy.

15.7 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Standardize a sample average. Independent losses have mean 40 and variance 900. Approximate the probability that their average over 225 losses exceeds 44.

The average has exact mean 40 and standard deviation \(30/15=2\). The standardized threshold is \((44-40)/2=2\), giving approximate tail \(1-\Phi(2)\approx0.02275\). No continuity correction applies unless a specific lattice structure is supplied. Finite variance and independence justify the asymptotic theorem, but do not certify this finite-sample error.

Exercise 2. Four integer boundaries. For an integer count B, translate B≤12, B<12, B≥12, and B>12 into continuity-corrected normal boundaries.

The first includes integer 12 and ends at 12.5. The second includes only through 11 and ends at 11.5. The third begins at 12 and uses lower boundary 11.5. The fourth begins at 13 and uses lower boundary 12.5. This derivation works regardless of the count's particular distribution, provided a normal approximation is otherwise appropriate.

Exercise 3. A finite-sample guarantee. Under iid losses with variance σ², how many observations suffice by Chebyshev to bound P(|average−μ|≥ε) by δ?

For \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(0<\delta<1\), require \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)\leq\delta\). Thus any integer \(n\geq\sigma^2/(\delta\varepsilon^2)\) suffices. This is sufficient, not generally necessary. Unlike a normal approximation, it holds for each such finite \(n\) under the assumptions.

Exercise 4. Dependence defeats averaging. Let Xi=V for every i, where V is Bernoulli with probability 1/2. Does the average converge in probability to 1/2?

The average equals \(V\) for every \(n\). For any \(0<\varepsilon<1/2\), \(P(|\overline X_n-1/2|\geq\varepsilon)=1\). The variance of the sum is \(n^2/4\), rather than \(n/4\), because every pair is perfectly dependent. Identical marginal distributions and finite variance do not replace independence.

Exercise 5. Exact normal sums. If the Xi are independent normal variables with common mean μ and variance σ²>0, when is the normal approximation exact?

The transform of the standardized sum is \(\prod_{i=1}^n\exp(-t^2/(2n))=\exp(-t^2/2)\). Thus it is standard normal for every positive integer \(n\), rather than only in a limit. There is no continuity correction because the sum is continuous. This conclusion uses the normal family in addition to the general central limit theorem assumptions.

15.8 Compare an approximation with an exact probability

The original program sums a binomial probability exactly up to floating-point arithmetic and compares corrected and uncorrected normal tails. It also confirms the four event boundaries algebraically. The comparison measures approximation error for one distribution and threshold; it does not validate all finite-sample central limit calculations.

"""Original finite-binomial benchmark for continuity correction."""
from math import comb, erfc, sqrt


def normal_tail(z):
    return erfc(z / sqrt(2)) / 2


n, p, k = 100, 0.1, 15
mean = n * p
sd = sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
exact = sum(comb(n, j) * p**j * (1 - p)**(n - j)
            for j in range(k, n + 1))
corrected = normal_tail((k - 0.5 - mean) / sd)
uncorrected = normal_tail((k - mean) / sd)
assert 0 < exact < 1
assert abs(corrected - exact) < abs(uncorrected - exact)
assert (k - 1) + 0.5 == k - 0.5
print(f"P(B >= {k}), B ~ Binomial({n}, {p})")
print(f"Exact finite sum: {exact:.8f}")
print(f"Normal, corrected: {corrected:.8f}")
print(f"Normal, uncorrected: {uncorrected:.8f}")
print(f"Corrected absolute error: {abs(corrected - exact):.8f}")
