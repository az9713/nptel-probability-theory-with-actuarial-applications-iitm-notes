18.1 A policy layer is an interval of loss

An insurer may cover losses above a deductible while refusing unlimited exposure to the largest events. The contract then transfers an interval of loss, called a layer. Its cost depends on how much probability lies above each point of that interval. This geometric description produces formulas that remain valid when the gross-loss distribution has atoms or infinite moments.

Let \(X\geq0\) be gross loss, \(d\geq0\) an ordinary deductible, \(u>0\) the width of the covered loss layer, and \(0<c\leq1\) the insurer's coinsurance fraction. Throughout this book,

\[Z=\min((X-d)_+,u),\qquad Y=cZ.\]

The payment cap is therefore \(cu\), with cap \(u\) before coinsurance. A ground-up exhaustion point, meaning the gross loss where this cap is reached, is \(d+u\). Contract documents sometimes use “limit” for that exhaustion point instead. Writing the payment function resolves the ambiguity. If an advertised maximum insurer payment is \(M\) after coinsurance, substitute \(u=M/c\).

Original worked example. With \(d=200,u=600,c=0.8\), losses 100, 500, and 1200 generate payments 0, 240, and 480. The layer is exhausted at gross loss 800, and the maximum payment is 480. Substituting 600 as a ground-up exhaustion point would describe a different contract and reduce the maximum payment to 320.

The layer also has the pathwise representation

\[Z=(X-d)_+-(X-d-u)_+.\]

For \(X\leq d\), both terms vanish. Between \(d\) and \(d+u\), only the first term remains. Above \(d+u\), subtraction leaves \(u\). This identity is useful for finite expected losses. For infinite expected losses, deriving the expectation through a bounded layer avoids subtracting infinity from infinity.

18.2 The payment distribution has two boundary atoms

Write \(F_X\) for the gross-loss cumulative distribution function. For \(0\leq y<cu\), the event \(Y\leq y\) is \(X\leq d+y/c\). At the cap every outcome qualifies. Therefore

\[F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}0,&y<0,\\F_X(d+y/c),&0\leq y<cu,\\1,&y\geq cu.\end{cases}\]

The zero atom is \(F_X(d)\). The cap atom is \(P(X\geq d+u)\), including any gross-loss atom at exhaustion. This is why writing \(P(X>d+u)\) without a continuity assumption can miss probability. When \(X\) is continuous, the interior density is \(f_X(d+y/c)/c\), the zero atom is \(F_X(d)\), and the cap atom is \(\overline F_X(d+u)\).

Original worked example. Suppose \(X\) is uniform on \((0,1000)\), with the contract above. Probability 0.2 maps to zero, probability 0.6 produces an interior payment, and probability 0.2 maps to 480. The interior density is \(1/800\) on \((0,480)\), which integrates to 0.6. The three components sum to one. A continuous density alone cannot describe this payment variable.

The per-payment distribution conditions on \(Y>0\), whose probability is \(q=P(X>d)\). For \(k>0\), its raw moment equals \(E[Y^k]/q\), provided \(q>0\). The cap atom's conditional probability is \(P(X\geq d+u)/q\). A deductible changes selection; a cap piles the selected large losses onto one payment amount.

18.3 Layer moments from survival probabilities

For \(0\leq t<u\), \(P(Z>t)=P(X>d+t)\). The tail-indicator identity from Chapter 17 therefore gives, for every real \(k>0\),

\[E[Y^k]=c^k k\int_0^u t^{k-1}\overline F_X(d+t)dt.\]

The result is finite because \(0\leq Y\leq cu\). Neither a gross-loss density nor a finite gross-loss mean is required. Endpoint values of the integrand do not affect the integral, but atoms at the endpoints still matter when specifying the complete distribution.

In particular,

\[m=E[Y]=c\int_d^{d+u}\overline F_X(x)dx,\qquad r=E[Y^2]=2c^2\int_0^u t\overline F_X(d+t)dt.\]

The variance is \(v=r-m^2\). The weighting by \(t\) in the second formula is essential: the second raw moment emphasizes upper portions of the layer. Squaring the expected layer length would give the square of the mean, which measures something different.

For the uniform example, \(\overline F_X(d+t)=0.8-t/1000\) over \(0\leq t\leq600\). Thus \(E[Z]=0.8(600)-600^2/2000=300\), while

\[E[Z^2]=2\left(0.8\frac{600^2}{2}-\frac{600^3}{3000}\right)=144000.\]

Hence \(m=240\), \(r=92160\), and \(v=34560\). Direct integration over the interior density plus the cap atom gives the same answer. For the mean it is \(\int_0^{480}y/800\,dy+0.2(480)=144+96=240\). The cap contribution is not a minor boundary correction; it supplies 40% of expected payment here.

18.4 Marginal prices of coverage

Changing a limit alters a narrow strip of covered loss. At continuity points, differentiation of the finite tail integral gives

\[\frac{\partial m}{\partial u}=c\overline F_X(d+u),\qquad \frac{\partial m}{\partial d}=c\{\overline F_X(d+u)-\overline F_X(d)\}.\]

Increasing layer width raises expected payment at a rate equal to coinsurance times exhaustion probability. Increasing the deductible while keeping layer width fixed shifts the entire interval upward. Its cost reduction is governed by the difference between attachment and exhaustion probabilities. These are derivatives of expected claims, before expenses or profit margins.

The derivative with respect to \(c\) is \(E[Z]\), and variance scales as \(c^2\operatorname{Var}(Z)\). These statements assume the pre-coinsurance layer width stays fixed. Holding the final payment cap \(M=cu\) fixed while varying \(c\) also changes \(u\), so the derivative is different. Comparative statics require specifying what remains constant.

A bound offers a quick consistency check. Since \(Y^2\leq cuY\),

\[0\leq\operatorname{Var}(Y)\leq m(cu-m).\]

The first inequality is the definition of variance. The second follows by taking the expectation of the pathwise inequality and subtracting \(m^2\). Equality in the upper bound occurs when payments take only zero and the cap. Thus the most variable bounded payment with a fixed mean concentrates probability at the endpoints. The uniform example satisfies \(34560\leq240(480-240)=57600\).

18.5 Stop-loss can attach to an annual aggregate

A stop-loss payment above retention \(a\geq0\) is \((W-a)_+\) for a specified nonnegative exposure \(W\). Taking \(W=X\) gives the uncapped ordinary-deductible payment for one loss. Taking \(W=S\), the total annual claims, gives an aggregate stop-loss contract. The transformation is identical; the modeled exposure and its distribution change.

If \(S\) has finite mean, its stop-loss premium is \(\pi_S(a)=E[(S-a)_+]=\int_a^\infty P(S>s)ds\). This expected claim cost is decreasing and convex in \(a\), by the same interval argument used for a deductible. The contract makes payment only after total annual claims exceed the retention, regardless of how claims were split into events.

Per-loss and aggregate protection cannot generally be interchanged. For any finite collection \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\geq0\),

\[\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-a)_+\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i-a\right)_+.\]

To see this, let \(A\) index terms exceeding \(a\). If \(A\) is empty, the left side is zero. Otherwise it is \(\sum_{i\in A}x_i-|A|a\), which is at most \(\sum_i x_i-a\), and hence at most its positive part. With two losses of 60 and retention 100, per-loss excess payments sum to zero, whereas aggregate excess is 20. This difference determines which risk a reinsurance arrangement transfers.

Finite layer coverage can restore finite insurer moments even when uncapped losses have infinite variance. This is a mathematical property of the specified payment cap. It does not establish that the cap is legally enforceable in every circumstance or that the portfolio is independent; those assumptions are outside the severity transformation.

18.6 An approximate normal stop-loss price

Original extension. If a justified aggregate approximation replaces \(S\) by \(G=\mu+\sigma Z\), where \(Z\) is standard normal and \(\sigma>0\), a stop-loss expectation can be calculated exactly for that surrogate. Put \(z=(a-\mu)/\sigma\), and let \(\phi\) and \(\Phi\) be the standard normal density and cumulative distribution function. Direct integration gives

\[E[(G-a)_+]=\int_z^\infty(\mu-a+\sigma t)\phi(t)dt=(\mu-a)[1-\Phi(z)]+\sigma\phi(z).\]

The hinge is \(\phi'(t)=-t\phi(t)\), so \(\int_z^\infty t\phi(t)dt=\phi(z)\). At retention \(a=\mu\), the surrogate price is \(\sigma/\sqrt{2\pi}\). For an original example with \(\mu=1000\) and \(\sigma=200\), this gives about 79.79 at retention 1000.

This expression is exact for \(G\), not for the nonnegative aggregate \(S\). A central limit theorem for distribution functions alone does not automatically establish convergence of unbounded expectations; appropriate moment control is also needed. A finite-sample normal stop-loss price therefore requires justification or numerical benchmarking beyond matching the first two moments. For \(\sigma=0\), use the deterministic payment \((\mu-a)_+\) directly.

18.7 Stacking layers and distinguishing censoring

Adjacent layers partition a covered loss interval. For \(u_1,u_2>0\), define \(Z_1=\min((X-d)_+,u_1)\) and \(Z_2=\min((X-d-u_1)_+,u_2)\). Checking losses below attachment, within either layer, and above both exhaustion points gives

\[Z_1+Z_2=\min((X-d)_+,u_1+u_2).\]

Expected layer costs therefore add. Variances require covariance because both layers respond to the same loss. In fact \(Z_1Z_2=u_1Z_2\) pathwise: whenever the upper layer pays, the lower layer is exhausted. Hence \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z_1,Z_2)=(u_1-E[Z_1])E[Z_2]\geq0\). This formula measures the dependence induced by stacking coverage on the same underlying loss.

As an original discrete example, let gross loss equal 0 or 300 with equal probabilities. Two layers of width 100 attach at 0 and 100. Both pay either 0 or 100 simultaneously. Each has mean 50 and variance 2500; covariance is 2500. Their total has variance 10000, not 5000. Layering allocates risk between parties, but does not make the layers independent.

A policy cap also differs from truncating a dataset. Capping maps each \(X\geq u\) to the payment \(u\), preserving the entire event probability as an atom. Conditioning on \(X<u\) discards upper-tail events and renormalizes the remaining distribution. For exponential mean \(\theta\), capped mean is \(\theta(1-e^{-u/\theta})\). The mean given \(X<u\) is instead \([\theta-(u+\theta)e^{-u/\theta}]/[1-e^{-u/\theta}]\), obtained by integrating \(xf_X(x)\) and dividing by selection probability. These are different quantities even though both involve the same boundary.

18.8 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Exponential layer moments. For exponential mean θ, derive the first two moments of Y=c min((X−d)+,u).

Put \(q=e^{-d/\theta}\) and \(b=u/\theta\). The mean is \(cq\int_0^ue^{-t/\theta}dt=cq\theta(1-e^{-b})\). Integration by parts gives \(\int_0^ut e^{-t/\theta}dt=\theta^2[1-(1+b)e^{-b}]\). Thus \(E[Y^2]=2c^2q\theta^2[1-(1+b)e^{-b}]\). Subtract the squared mean for variance. As \(u\to\infty\), these reduce to the uncapped deductible moments.

Exercise 2. An atom at exhaustion. Gross loss equals 0, 2, and 5 with probabilities 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5. Let d=1,u=4,c=1/2. Determine the complete payment law.

The mapped values are 0, 0.5, and 2, with unchanged probabilities. The cap atom is 0.5 because loss 5 is exactly the exhaustion point. Using \(P(X>5)\) would incorrectly give zero. The mean is 1.15 and second moment is 2.075, so variance is \(2.075-1.15^2=0.7525\).

Exercise 3. Compare two placements of the same layer. If the survival function is decreasing, prove that shifting a layer of fixed width upward cannot increase expected payment.

For \(d_2\geq d_1\), survival satisfies \(\overline F_X(d_2+t)\leq\overline F_X(d_1+t)\) for every \(t\geq0\). Integrate from zero to \(u\) and multiply by \(c>0\). This proves the claim without differentiability. Strict inequality requires survival to decrease on a subset of positive length within the shifted comparison; an interval with no loss probability can yield equal prices.

Exercise 4. A gross loss without a mean. Suppose P(X>x)=(1+x) to power −1/2. Find the mean payment for d=3,u=5,c=1 and explain its finiteness.

The mean is \(\int_3^8(1+x)^{-1/2}dx=2(\sqrt9-\sqrt4)=2\). The gross-loss mean is infinite because the same antiderivative diverges at infinity. The bounded payment is at most 5, so its moments of every positive integer order are finite. A bounded layer integral is valid directly; a difference of two infinite stop-loss expectations is not.

Exercise 5. Aggregate stop-loss versus an annual cap. Annual claims equal 40 or 160 with equal probabilities. Compare stop-loss above 100 with insurer payment capped at 100.

Stop-loss payment \((S-100)_+\) takes values 0 and 60, so its mean is 30. Capped payment \(\min(S,100)\) takes values 40 and 100, so its mean is 70. The variables sum pathwise to \(S\); their means sum to 100, the expected annual claims. The word “limit” alone cannot identify which of these contracts is intended.

18.9 A numerical integral checked against a discrete law

The original program evaluates exponential layer moments analytically and checks the tail-integral formula on a discrete loss distribution by exact integration of its stepwise survival function. It also tests the bounded-variable variance inequality. Both distributional endpoints are retained.

"""Original layer check using exact step-survival integration."""
from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import exp

losses = [(0, F(1, 5)), (2, F(3, 10)), (5, F(1, 2))]
d, u, c = 1, 4, F(1, 2)
law = [(c * min(max(x - d, 0), u), p) for x, p in losses]
mean = sum(y * p for y, p in law)
second = sum(y**2 * p for y, p in law)
tail_mean = F(0)
tail_second = F(0)
for x, p in losses:
    width = min(max(x - d, 0), u)
    tail_mean += c * p * width
    tail_second += c**2 * p * width**2
assert mean == tail_mean == F(23, 20)
assert second == tail_second == F(83, 40)
variance = second - mean**2
assert 0 <= variance <= mean * (c * u - mean)
print(f"Discrete layer mean: {float(mean):.6f}")
print(f"Discrete layer second moment: {float(second):.6f}")
print(f"Discrete layer variance: {float(variance):.6f}")
theta, d, u, c = 2.0, 1.0, 2.0, 0.5
q, b = exp(-d / theta), u / theta
m = c * q * theta * (1 - exp(-b))
r = 2 * c**2 * q * theta**2 * (1 - (1 + b) * exp(-b))
assert 0 < r < c * u * m
print(f"Exponential layer mean, second moment: {m:.6f}, {r:.6f}")
