8.1 When diversification changes risk

Two portfolios can have identical individual loss distributions and very different aggregate risk. Suppose two one-year payments, measured in thousands of currency units, each have variance 100. If the payments move together exactly, their sum has variance 400. If they are independent, its variance is 200. Pricing each policy from its own distribution therefore leaves an essential part of the portfolio model unspecified: the joint behavior of payments.

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be real random variables on the same probability space, with finite second moments. Write \(\mu_X=E[X]\), \(\mu_Y=E[Y]\), \(\sigma_X^2=E[(X-\mu_X)^2]\), and define \(\sigma_Y^2\) similarly. The finite-moment assumptions ensure that all products used below are integrable. Indeed, \(2|XY|\leq X^2+Y^2\). A covariance is a property of the joint distribution, whereas a variance needs only one marginal distribution.

The central question is how much of aggregate uncertainty comes from each marginal and how much comes from their interaction. The answer is exact for second moments. It does not, by itself, specify a probability of ruin or a tail quantile. Different joint laws can share the same covariance and still assign different probabilities to simultaneous extreme losses.

8.2 Centering reveals the interaction

The covariance is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]\). Positive products arise when both deviations have the same sign. Negative products arise when one variable is above its mean and the other below. Expansion and linearity give the computational identity

\[\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y].\]

This identity does not assume independence. Independence supplies a sufficient condition for zero covariance: for independent integrable variables, the joint expectation factors into the product of expectations. In a finite table this is the calculation \(\sum_x\sum_y xy\,p_X(x)p_Y(y)=(\sum_x xp_X(x))(\sum_y yp_Y(y))\). The corresponding integral factors in the continuous case.

As an original finite example, give \((X,Y)\) the values \((0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\) with probabilities \(0.4,0.1,0.1,0.4\). Both means equal \(0.5\), both variances equal \(0.25\), and \(E[XY]=0.4\). Consequently their covariance is \(0.15\). Joint claim occurrence has probability \(0.4\), exceeding the independent benchmark \(0.25\).

Covariance changes with the measurement scale. For real constants \(a,b,c,d\), centering \(aX+b\) gives \(a(X-\mu_X)\), so \(\operatorname{Cov}(aX+b,cY+d)=ac\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\). Adding a fixed premium or reserve does not change covariance. Expressing both losses in currency units rather than thousands multiplies covariance by a million. Its unit is the product of the two variables' units.

Zero covariance does not establish independence. Let \(X\) be uniform on \([-1,1]\) and set \(Y=X^2\). Symmetry gives \(E[X]=E[X^3]=0\), so \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0\). Nevertheless \(Y\) is determined by \(X\). More explicitly, \(P(|X|\leq1/2,Y\leq1/4)=1/2\), while the product of the two marginal probabilities is \(1/4\). The failure is nonlinear: positive and negative values of \(X\) produce the same squared value.

8.3 Correlation and its exact equality cases

When both variances are positive, define the dimensionless correlation by \(\rho_{XY}=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)\). It measures standardized linear association. If either variable is constant almost surely, its standard deviation is zero and correlation is undefined; assigning it zero conceals this division by zero.

To prove the bound \(-1\leq\rho_{XY}\leq1\), standardize the variables as \(U=(X-\mu_X)/\sigma_X\) and \(V=(Y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y\). Both have mean zero and second moment one. For every real \(a\), nonnegativity of a square gives

\[0\leq E[(U-aV)^2]=1-2a\rho_{XY}+a^2.\]

Choose \(a=\rho_{XY}\). The right-hand side becomes \(1-\rho_{XY}^2\), proving the bound. Equality requires a nonnegative random variable to have expectation zero, hence \(U-\rho_{XY}V=0\) almost surely. Thus correlation is exactly one or minus one precisely when the variables have an almost-sure affine relationship of positive or negative slope, respectively. Merely having a monotone nonlinear relationship does not force correlation to equal one.

For the finite claim indicators above, correlation is \(0.15/0.25=0.6\). It is positive but far from a deterministic relationship: each off-diagonal event still has probability \(0.1\). Under a change of units, positive scale factors leave correlation unchanged; changing the sign of exactly one variable reverses it. This follows directly from the covariance scaling identity and \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\sigma_X^2\).

8.4 Portfolio variance and common shocks

For constants \(a,b\), expand the centered square of \(aX+bY\). Taking expectations yields

\[\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).\]

For a portfolio \(S=\sum_{i=1}^n a_iX_i\), expansion of the square contains one term for every ordered pair. Combining the symmetric pairs gives \(\operatorname{Var}(S)=\sum_i a_i^2\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+2\sum_{i<j}a_ia_j\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\). Pairwise zero covariance suffices to remove the cross terms. Mutual independence is stronger than this variance calculation needs.

Suppose all losses have variance \(\sigma^2\), and every distinct pair has correlation \(\rho\). With equal exposure weights, the average loss \(\bar X=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i\) has variance

\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2\left(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{n}\right).\]

The diagonal contribution is \(n\sigma^2/n^2\); the \(n(n-1)\) ordered off-diagonal terms contribute \(n(n-1)\rho\sigma^2/n^2\). For \(n=100\), \(\sigma^2=100\), and \(\rho=0.05\), the average variance is \(5.95\), compared with one under independence. A small common dependence leaves a substantial residual risk.

A concrete mechanism is \(X_i=Z+\varepsilon_i\), where the common shock \(Z\) and all idiosyncratic errors \(\varepsilon_i\) are mutually independent with finite variances. Then \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\operatorname{Var}(Z)\) for distinct indices, and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\operatorname{Var}(Z)+\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_1)/n\) when errors share a variance. Increasing the portfolio size reduces the second component, but leaves the common component intact.

Not every proposed correlation array is possible. If \(\Sigma\) is the covariance matrix with entries \(\Sigma_{ij}=\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\), then \(a^T\Sigma a=\operatorname{Var}(\sum_i a_iX_i)\geq0\) for every vector \(a\). Such a matrix is called positive semidefinite. In the equal-correlation example, the all-ones vector implies \(1+(n-1)\rho\geq0\); contrasts between two coordinates imply \(\rho\leq1\). Therefore \(-1/(n-1)\leq\rho\leq1\). A fixed negative correlation cannot persist across arbitrarily large exchangeable portfolios.

8.5 What variance leaves unresolved

Covariance calculations need finite second moments. A severity distribution with infinite variance has no finite standard deviation, so ordinary correlation and the portfolio variance formulas cannot supply a finite risk measure. Even with finite variance, the mean and covariance do not determine the distribution of a sum. A tail-sensitive decision needs a joint model or additional inequalities and assumptions.

There is also a distinction between mathematical and empirical covariance. The expectation above belongs to a probability model. A covariance computed from past annual observations estimates that model quantity under assumptions about sampling and stability. Changing weather exposure, inflation, or claim reporting can change the joint distribution. A deterministic code check of a finite table establishes arithmetic for that table; it does not validate a fitted catastrophe model.

A second finite example shows why tail behavior cannot be recovered from covariance alone. Let \(B\) be equally likely zero or one, and let \(H\) be an independent sign, equally likely minus one or one. Set \(X=H\) and \(Y=H\) when \(B=1\), but \(Y=-H\) when \(B=0\). Both variables are symmetric signs, and their covariance is zero. In this particular construction the four sign pairs are equally likely, so the variables are independent. The calculation gives one joint model compatible with zero covariance, rather than proving independence from zero covariance in general.

Contrast it with \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1,0,1\}\) and \(Y=X^2\). Here \(E[X]=0\), \(E[Y]=2/3\), and \(E[XY]=0\), again giving covariance zero. Yet the event \(X=0\) is exactly the event \(Y=0\), with probability \(1/3\), while an independent coupling of the same marginals would give their intersection probability \(1/9\). Replacing this dependent coupling by independent draws preserves marginal means, marginal variances, and covariance, but changes joint event probabilities. This is the finite example checked in the program.

Covariance also determines the best linear predictor when \(\operatorname{Var}(X)>0\). To predict \(Y\) by \(a+bX\), first choose \(a=\mu_Y-b\mu_X\), eliminating the mean prediction error. The remaining mean squared error is \(\sigma_Y^2-2b\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+b^2\sigma_X^2\). Completing the square minimizes it at \(b=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/\sigma_X^2\). The minimum is \(\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho_{XY}^2)\) when \(\sigma_Y>0\). This is optimal among affine predictors only. In the squared example, the best linear predictor is constant even though a nonlinear predictor is exact.

The available tools now answer the opening question exactly at the level of second moments. Marginal variances determine the individual terms, and covariance determines every pairwise contribution. Independence is a convenient sufficient condition for diversification, but absence of linear covariance is weaker and says little about joint extremes. Choosing a dependence model therefore requires specifying more than a single correlation whenever the decision depends on nonlinear payouts or extreme outcomes.

8.6 Exercises with worked solutions

Each calculation should identify whether it uses a joint law, independence, or only a covariance value. This is more than bookkeeping: a variance identity can remain valid when a distributional assertion fails. In particular, verify any claim of independence by factorizing probabilities or densities, rather than checking a single numerical covariance. For a portfolio calculation, include all exposure weights before summing pairwise contributions.

Exercise 1. Compute the mean and variance of the sum of the two claim indicators in Section 8.2.

Linearity gives \(E[X+Y]=1\). The variance identity gives \(0.25+0.25+2(0.15)=0.8\). Directly, the sum is 0, 1, or 2 with probabilities 0.4, 0.2, 0.4. Its second moment is \(0.2+4(0.4)=1.8\), so its variance is \(1.8-1^2=0.8\).

Exercise 2. If Var(X)=4, Var(Y)=9, and correlation is −1/2, find Var(2X−Y).

The covariance is \((-1/2)(2)(3)=-3\). Expanding the linear combination gives \(4(4)+9+2(2)(-1)(-3)=37\). Subtracting a negatively associated variable increases the variance here.

Exercise 3. For X uniform on [−1,1], compute the variance of Y=X² and verify that its zero covariance is nondegenerate.

Integration gives \(E[X^{2k}]=1/(2k+1)\). Thus \(E[Y]=1/3\), \(E[Y^2]=1/5\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1/5-1/9=4/45\). Also \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=1/3\). Both variances are positive; symmetry gives zero covariance, so their correlation is actually zero despite dependence.

Exercise 4. Can four standardized losses all have pairwise correlation −0.4?

If they did, the sum would have variance \(4+2\binom42(-0.4)=4-4.8=-0.8\), contradicting nonnegativity. The admissible equal-correlation lower bound is \(-1/3\). Pairwise admissibility alone does not guarantee joint admissibility.

Exercise 5. Independent payments have variances 4 and 9. Find the weight w minimizing the variance of wX+(1−w)Y.

The variance is \(4w^2+9(1-w)^2=13w^2-18w+9\). Completing the square gives \(13(w-9/13)^2+36/13\), so \(w=9/13\) and the minimum variance is \(36/13\). This minimizes variance under the specified weighting constraint, without imposing an expected-return or premium constraint.

8.7 An exact finite-table check

The program enumerates the original joint table using rational arithmetic. It checks the aggregate identity and a dependent, uncorrelated pair. These are finite examples, separate from the general proofs.

"""Exact covariance and portfolio-variance examples."""
from fractions import Fraction as F


table = [(0, 0, F(2, 5)), (0, 1, F(1, 10)),
         (1, 0, F(1, 10)), (1, 1, F(2, 5))]
mean_x = sum(x*p for x, y, p in table)
mean_y = sum(y*p for x, y, p in table)
var_x = sum((x-mean_x)**2*p for x, y, p in table)
var_y = sum((y-mean_y)**2*p for x, y, p in table)
cov = sum((x-mean_x)*(y-mean_y)*p for x, y, p in table)
var_sum = sum((x+y-mean_x-mean_y)**2*p for x, y, p in table)
assert sum(p for x, y, p in table) == 1
assert var_sum == var_x + var_y + 2*cov == F(4, 5)
values = [-1, 0, 1]
mean_square = sum(F(x*x, 3) for x in values)
uncorrelated = sum(F(x, 3)*(x*x-mean_square) for x in values)
assert uncorrelated == 0
assert F(1, 3) != F(1, 3)*F(1, 3)  # X=0 and X^2=0 coincide.
print(f"covariance = {cov}; sum variance = {var_sum}")
print(f"dependent square example covariance = {uncorrelated}")
print(f"100-policy average variance = {100*(.05+.95/100):.2f}")

covariance = 3/20; sum variance = 4/5
dependent square example covariance = 0
100-policy average variance = 5.95
