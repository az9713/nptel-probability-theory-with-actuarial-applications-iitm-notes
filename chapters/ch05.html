5.1 Count exposures or count events?

A portfolio contains a fixed number of one-year exposures. A model records whether each exposure produces at least one claim. Another model records every claim, allowing several from one exposure. These questions produce different count variables. A Bernoulli indicator and its binomial sum describe the first construction; a Poisson count can describe an unbounded number of events. Choosing a family requires specifying what one count represents.

A Bernoulli variable \(I\) with parameter \(p\in[0,1]\) equals one with probability \(p\) and zero with probability \(1-p\). It can represent a claim occurrence indicator for one exposure. Since \(I^2=I\), its mean is \(p\) and variance is \(p-p^2=p(1-p)\). The variance is zero at the deterministic endpoints and reaches its maximum one-quarter at \(p=1/2\), as follows from \(p(1-p)=1/4-(p-1/2)^2\).

Independence of indicators is a modeling assumption. Two contracts may share an underlying event source, and a classification scheme may assign different occurrence probabilities to different contracts. Neither equal probabilities nor independence follows from the fact that contracts are separately labeled. The probability formulas below state these hypotheses explicitly.

5.2 A binomial count from independent equal-probability trials

Let \(I_1,\ldots,I_n\) be Bernoulli variables with the same parameter \(p\), and assume the events \(\{I_j=1\}\) are mutually independent. Complementation then permits every pattern of zeros and ones to factor into marginal probabilities; this is mutual independence of these indicators. Define \(N=\sum_{j=1}^n I_j\). Then \(N\) has the binomial distribution with parameters \(n,p\). Its support is \(0,1,\ldots,n\). For any fixed pattern with \(k\) ones, independence gives probability \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\). There are \(\binom nk\) disjoint patterns containing exactly \(k\) ones. Hence

\[P(N=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,\ldots,n.\]

The binomial theorem confirms normalization: summing over \(k\) gives \([p+(1-p)]^n=1\). At \(p=0\) all mass is at zero, and at \(p=1\) all mass is at \(n\); interpret the formula through these limiting laws rather than assigning a problematic meaning to \(0^0\) in an implementation.

Linearity gives \(E[N]=np\). To derive variance, expand \(N^2=\sum_jI_j+2\sum_{i\lt j}I_iI_j\). Independence gives \(E[I_iI_j]=p^2\) for distinct indices, so \(E[N^2]=np+n(n-1)p^2\). Subtracting \((np)^2\) gives \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=np(1-p)\). This derivation identifies exactly where independence enters. Linearity alone proves the mean but cannot remove the cross terms in the second moment.

For five independent exposures, each with occurrence probability 0.1, the probability of exactly two claim-producing exposures is \(\binom52(0.1)^2(0.9)^3=0.0729\). The probability of at least one is \(1-(0.9)^5=0.40951\). The count mean is 0.5 and variance 0.45. A count mean below one does not mean a claim is impossible; it averages zero, one, and higher counts.

With independent unequal probabilities \(p_j\), the mean remains \(\sum_jp_j\), but the equal-pattern argument fails. For two exposures with probabilities 0.1 and 0.3, \(P(N=2)=0.03\). Replacing both probabilities by their average 0.2 would give 0.04. An average occurrence probability preserves the mean count, not the entire distribution.

5.3 The Poisson law and its moments

A Poisson variable with parameter \(\lambda\gt0\) has probability mass

\[P(N=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.\]

The parameter \(\lambda\) is the expected count over the specified observation window, not necessarily a rate per unit time. If a constant rate \(r\) is measured per year and the window has duration \(t\) years, the corresponding mean count is \(\lambda=rt\). A Poisson process would also specify independent counts in disjoint intervals and their time scaling; a one-window Poisson distribution alone does not supply those assumptions.

Normalization follows from the exponential series \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\lambda^k/k!=e^\lambda\). For the mean, cancel \(k/k!=1/(k-1)!\), shift the summation index, and obtain

\[E[N]=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k\lambda^k}{k!}=\lambda e^{-\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^j}{j!}=\lambda.\]

Likewise \(E[N(N-1)]=\lambda^2\). Since \(N^2=N(N-1)+N\), its second moment is \(\lambda^2+\lambda\), giving variance \(\lambda\). Equality of mean and variance is a structural restriction of this family. It is a mathematical property of the model; seeing similar sample values is not a proof that a data-generating mechanism is Poisson.

For \(\lambda=2\), zero events have probability \(e^{-2}\), approximately 0.135335. Exactly two have probability \(2e^{-2}\), approximately 0.270671. At least one has probability \(1-e^{-2}\). The count has no finite upper bound, although large counts become unlikely. A fixed exposure-indicator count cannot exceed the number of exposures, so a Poisson approximation changes that support.

Successive masses satisfy \(p_{k+1}=p_k\lambda/(k+1)\), obtained by taking their ratio. This recurrence avoids repeated factorial evaluations for moderate parameters and makes the mode visible: masses increase while \(k+1\lt\lambda\) and decrease after \(k+1\gt\lambda\). For large \(\lambda\), starting from \(e^{-\lambda}\) can underflow numerically; a more careful implementation starts near the mode or uses log probabilities. The chapter’s small parameter avoids that issue.

5.4 A rare-event limit, with its assumptions visible

Fix \(\lambda\gt0\), and let \(N_n\) be binomial with \(n\) trials and probability \(\lambda/n\), for positive integers \(n\ge\lambda\). The mean remains \(\lambda\) while each trial becomes rare. Fix a nonnegative integer \(k\). For all sufficiently large \(n\), specifically \(n\gt\lambda\) and \(n\ge k\), its mass can be rewritten as

\[P(N_n=k)=\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac jn\right)\right]\left(1-\frac\lambda n\right)^n\left(1-\frac\lambda n\right)^{-k}.\]

For fixed \(k\), the finite product tends to one, the last factor tends to one, and the middle factor tends to \(e^{-\lambda}\). The middle limit follows by taking logarithms and using \(\log(1-u)/u\to-1\) as \(u\to0\). Therefore each fixed count probability tends to \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\). Summing finitely many such probabilities proves convergence of the CDF at every fixed noninteger threshold.

This limit explains a Poisson approximation for many independent trials with small common occurrence probability and bounded expected count. It does not say that every low-frequency dependent count is approximately Poisson. If all \(n\) indicators equal one common Bernoulli variable with probability \(\lambda/n\), their sum is either zero or \(n\). Its mean is still \(\lambda\), but its zero probability tends to one rather than \(e^{-\lambda}\). Independence changes the limit completely.

The original program compares the binomial and Poisson masses at zero and two for \(n=1000,p=0.002\). It also sums forty Poisson masses and checks truncated first and second moments. The displayed normalization is numerically close to one because the omitted tail is tiny for this parameter; it is not an exact statement that support ends at 39.

"""Binomial rare-event approximation and truncated Poisson moments."""
from math import comb, exp, factorial, isclose

n = 1000
rate = 2.0
p = rate/n
exact_zero = (1 - p)**n
poisson_zero = exp(-rate)
mass = [exp(-rate)*rate**k/factorial(k) for k in range(40)]
assert isclose(sum(mass), 1.0, abs_tol=1e-14)
mean = sum(k*q for k, q in enumerate(mass))
variance = sum((k - rate)**2*q for k, q in enumerate(mass))
assert isclose(mean, rate, abs_tol=1e-12)
assert isclose(variance, rate, abs_tol=1e-12)
binomial_two = comb(n, 2)*p**2*(1 - p)**(n - 2)
print(f"P(N=0): binomial {exact_zero:.8f}; Poisson {poisson_zero:.8f}")
print(f"P(N=2): binomial {binomial_two:.8f}; Poisson {mass[2]:.8f}")
print(f"Poisson truncated mean {mean:.6f}; variance {variance:.6f}")

P(N=2): binomial 0.27094160; Poisson 0.27067057
Poisson truncated mean 2.000000; variance 2.000000

A useful diagnostic is to compare the support, the unit of observation, and the implied mean-variance relation before computing a tail probability. Bernoulli, binomial, and Poisson laws answer related questions under different assumptions. None is selected by the word “claim” alone.

5.5 Adding and classifying Poisson counts

Independent Poisson counts add within the same family. Let \(N_1,N_2\) have parameters \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\), and assume events involving the first count are independent of events involving the second. For a nonnegative integer \(k\), partition \(N_1+N_2=k\) according to \(N_1=j\), where \(j=0,\ldots,k\). Multiplication and addition give

\[P(N_1+N_2=k)=e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}\sum_{j=0}^k\frac{\lambda_1^j\lambda_2^{k-j}}{j!(k-j)!}=e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}\frac{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^k}{k!}.\]

The last equality is the finite binomial theorem after multiplying and dividing by \(k!\). This proves the family closure directly, without a generating function. Two independent channels with expected counts ten and three therefore have total count Poisson with parameter thirteen. If instead both channel counts equal one common Poisson variable, their sum has only even values. The independence hypothesis cannot be removed.

Another original extension classifies each event independently as marked with probability \(q\). Suppose the total count \(N\) is Poisson with parameter \(\lambda\), and conditional on \(N=n\), the marked count \(M\) is binomial with parameters \(n,q\). For \(0\lt q\lt1\), total probability and the substitution \(n=k+j\) give

\[P(M=k)=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\binom nkq^k(1-q)^{n-k}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}=e^{-\lambda}\frac{(\lambda q)^k}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{[\lambda(1-q)]^j}{j!}=e^{-\lambda q}\frac{(\lambda q)^k}{k!}.\]

Thus the marked count is Poisson with parameter \(\lambda q\); the endpoint cases follow by deterministic classification. This operation is called thinning. It requires an explicit independent classification rule conditional on the total. If all events in a year share one random mark decision, the conditional count is not binomial and the result fails. A uniform reduction in expected frequency therefore does not by itself determine a thinned distribution.

These two constructions separate two practical operations: adding independent event sources and selecting independently marked events from one source. Both preserve a Poisson family under their stated hypotheses, but they change its parameter in different ways. Superposition adds expected counts; thinning multiplies one expected count by the retention probability.

5.6 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. A Bernoulli claim indicator has mean 0.04. Find its parameter and variance.

A claim indicator has mean 0.04. Its success probability is therefore \(p=0.04\), and its variance is \(0.04(0.96)=0.0384\). The mean determines the whole law because the support is restricted to zero and one.

Exercise 2. For twenty independent indicators with probability 0.05, derive P(N<=1), mean, and variance.

For twenty independent claim indicators with common probability 0.05, derive \(P(N\le1)\), the mean, and variance. The probability is \((0.95)^{20}+20(0.05)(0.95)^{19}\). The mean is one and variance 0.95. Both zero- and one-count patterns must be included.

Exercise 3. A Poisson count has zero probability 0.8. Find its parameter and probability of one event.

If a Poisson count has \(P(N=0)=0.8\), then \(e^{-\lambda}=0.8\), so \(\lambda=-\log0.8\), approximately 0.223144. Thus \(P(N=1)=0.8(-\log0.8)\), approximately 0.178515. A zero probability identifies the parameter within this family.

Exercise 4. Derive the positive-count conditional PMF and mean for a Poisson count.

For Poisson \(N\) with parameter \(\lambda\gt0\), find \(P(N=k\mid N\gt0)\), \(k\ge1\). Divide each positive mass by \(1-e^{-\lambda}\). The conditional mean is \(\lambda/(1-e^{-\lambda})\), because zero contributes nothing to the unconditional mean. This is a zero-truncated law, not an ordinary Poisson law.

Exercise 5. A count is zero with probability 0.9 and ten with probability 0.1. Compare its moments with a mean-one Poisson law.

A count equals zero with probability 0.9 and ten with probability 0.1. Its mean is one, second moment ten, and variance nine. A Poisson law with the same mean has variance one and positive mass at every nonnegative integer. Matching a mean cannot identify a distribution.
