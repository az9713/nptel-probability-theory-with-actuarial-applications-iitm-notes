12.1 A premium can depend on what is known

An insurer may know a policyholder's risk class before setting a premium, while the eventual payment remains random. The mean loss within a class is therefore a different object from the unconditional mean across the portfolio. Conditional expectation makes this distinction precise. Its variance identity then separates randomness within classes from variation between class means.

Let \(X\) denote a real payment with \(E[|X|]<\infty\), and let \(Z\) denote an observed risk variable. For discrete \(Z\), the conditional mean at a value \(z\) with positive probability is \(m(z)=E[X\mid Z=z]=\sum_x xP(X=x\mid Z=z)\), replacing the sum by an integral when the conditional law has a density. The function \(m\) converts a class label into a number. The random variable \(m(Z)\), written \(E[X\mid Z]\), is the class mean associated with the randomly selected policyholder.

The notation distinguishes \(E[X\mid Z=z]\), a number for a specified observation, from \(E[X\mid Z]\), a random variable before that observation is supplied. This distinction is essential when taking an expectation or variance of a conditional mean. A single class mean has no portfolio variance until the class itself is randomized.

12.2 Conditional means preserve group totals

For a general observation \(Z\), a conditional expectation \(M=E[X\mid Z]\) is an integrable random variable determined by \(Z\) such that \(E[X\mathbf1_A]=E[M\mathbf1_A]\) for every event \(A\) determined by \(Z\). Events determined by \(Z\) form the collection \(\sigma(Z)\), called the sigma-field generated by \(Z\). The defining equality says that replacing each payment by its conditional mean preserves the expected total in every observable group.

For a finite partition into classes, the definition follows directly from \(m(z)P(Z=z)=E[X\mathbf1_{\{Z=z\}}]\). Adding these identities over any collection of classes gives the group-total property. For continuously distributed \(Z\), a conditional density provides one construction: \(m(z)=\int x f_{X\mid Z}(x\mid z)dx\) wherever the marginal density of \(Z\) is positive. Values of \(m\) on a set of \(Z\)-probability zero do not affect \(m(Z)\), so conditional expectation is defined up to almost-sure equality.

As an original model, suppose 80 percent of policyholders are in class \(L\) and 20 percent in class \(H\). A unit claim indicator \(X\) has probabilities \(0.1\) and \(0.4\) in the respective classes. Then \(m(L)=0.1\), \(m(H)=0.4\), and \(E[X\mid Z]\) takes those two values with probabilities \(0.8\) and \(0.2\). Observing the class changes the conditional distribution without determining the claim outcome.

If \(h(Z)\) is observable and the required products are integrable, it can be taken outside conditioning: \(E[h(Z)X\mid Z]=h(Z)E[X\mid Z]\). For a bounded class-dependent multiplier this follows by checking each class and then approximating general measurable multipliers by simple functions. This is not a general factorization rule. In particular, \(E[XY\mid Z]\) need not equal \(E[X\mid Z]E[Y\mid Z]\) unless additional conditional independence or another sufficient condition holds.

12.3 Average the conditional mean

An original continuous example makes the conditioning operation explicit. Let \((Z,X)\) have density two on \(0<z<x<1\). This triangle has area one half, so the joint density integrates to one. Integrating over \(x\) gives \(f_Z(z)=2(1-z)\). Dividing the joint density by this marginal yields \(f_{X\mid Z}(x\mid z)=1/(1-z)\) on \((z,1)\). Thus the conditional law is uniform on an interval whose lower endpoint is observed.

Its conditional mean is \(m(z)=\int_z^1x\,dx/(1-z)=(1+z)/2\), and conditional variance is \((1-z)^2/12\). The formulas depend on the observed value even though the joint density is constant. Averaging the conditional mean gives \(\int_0^1[(1+z)/2]2(1-z)dz=2/3\). Direct marginalization gives \(f_X(x)=2x\) and the same mean \(\int_0^1 2x^2dx=2/3\). The zero-probability endpoint \(Z=1\) need not be assigned a unique conditional density.

Taking the entire sample space as the group \(A\) in the defining equality proves the law of total expectation:

\[E[X]=E[E[X\mid Z]].\]

In the two-class model, this yields \(E[X]=0.8(0.1)+0.2(0.4)=0.16\). Since \(X\) is an indicator, this is also the unconditional claim probability. For a continuous mixing variable with density \(f_Z\), the same law reads \(E[X]=\int m(z)f_Z(z)dz\). Integrability permits the signed integrals to be interchanged; for nonnegative \(X\), the identity also holds with infinite values using nonnegative integration.

A more general tower property requires nested information. If \(\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H\) are sigma-fields, meaning that every event observable with \(\mathcal G\) is also observable with \(\mathcal H\), then \(E[E[X\mid\mathcal H]\mid\mathcal G]=E[X\mid\mathcal G]\). To prove it, the left side is \(\mathcal G\)-measurable, and its expected total over any \(A\in\mathcal G\) equals \(E[E[X\mid\mathcal H]\mathbf1_A]=E[X\mathbf1_A]\), since \(A\in\mathcal H\). It therefore has the defining property of the right side.

For example, detailed information might include both region and vehicle type, while coarse information includes only region. Averaging the detailed conditional mean within each region recovers the region's conditional mean. Two unrelated observations need not be nested. Replacing the fine information by an arbitrary variable and invoking a tower can therefore fail; the containment of event collections is the mathematical hypothesis doing the work.

12.4 Within-class and between-class uncertainty

Assume now \(E[X^2]<\infty\). Let \(M=E[X\mid Z]\), let \(\mu=E[X]\), and define conditional variance as \(\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z)=E[(X-M)^2\mid Z]\). The residual \(R=X-M\) has conditional mean zero: \(E[R\mid Z]=M-M=0\). Decompose the centered payment as \(X-\mu=R+(M-\mu)\), square, and take expectation.

The cross term vanishes because \(E[R(M-\mu)]=E[(M-\mu)E[R\mid Z]]=0\). These products are integrable by the finite-second-moment assumption. The two remaining terms give the law of total variance:

\[\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z)]+\operatorname{Var}(E[X\mid Z]).\]

The first term is average uncertainty remaining after the class is observed. The second is variation of the class means before observation. Neither term is generally zero. Omitting the second term underestimates portfolio variance whenever conditional means differ.

In the class-indicator example, conditional variances are \(0.1(0.9)=0.09\) and \(0.4(0.6)=0.24\). Their average is \(0.8(0.09)+0.2(0.24)=0.12\). The variance of the conditional mean is \(0.8(0.1-0.16)^2+0.2(0.4-0.16)^2=0.0144\). Their sum is \(0.1344\), matching the unconditional Bernoulli variance \(0.16(0.84)\). The class label explains part of the variance but leaves most of the claim uncertainty unresolved.

The identity also has a conditional version. For nested information \(\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H\), square the same residual decomposition around \(E[X\mid\mathcal G]\) and condition throughout on \(\mathcal G\). The mixed term is zero by the tower property, giving \(\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)=E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal H)\mid\mathcal G]+\operatorname{Var}(E[X\mid\mathcal H]\mid\mathcal G)\), almost surely. For a portfolio divided first by region, then by detailed classes within region, this splits uncertainty separately inside each region.

12.5 Conditional expectation minimizes squared error

A conditional mean is the best predictor among functions of the observed information when prediction error is measured by mean squared error. Let \(a(Z)\) be any square-integrable predictor, and retain \(M=E[X\mid Z]\). Write \(X-a(Z)=(X-M)+(M-a(Z))\). Expanding as above and using the zero conditional mean residual yields

\[E[(X-a(Z))^2]=E[(X-M)^2]+E[(M-a(Z))^2].\]

The second term is nonnegative, so \(a=M\) minimizes error. Equality occurs only when \(a(Z)=M\) almost surely. The proof identifies what optimality means: it is relative to the information \(Z\), the model distribution, and squared-error loss. Absolute-error prediction generally calls for a conditional median instead, while a tail-sensitive insurance objective can call for a conditional quantile.

With no class information, the best constant predictor is \(\mu=0.16\), with squared error \(0.1344\). Using the class-dependent predictor reduces squared error to \(0.12\). The reduction is \(\operatorname{Var}(M)=0.0144\), precisely the between-class term. Information improves prediction without changing the underlying unconditional variance of the actual claim.

Conditional expectation is not a causal statement. A class label may predict claims because it is associated with an unobserved exposure or reporting pattern. Changing the label alone need not change the loss. The mathematical calculation only asserts relationships inside the specified joint distribution of \((X,Z)\).

The same residual argument gives an orthogonality statement useful in regression. For every square-integrable function \(h(Z)\), \(E[(X-M)h(Z)]=0\), whenever the product is integrable. Condition on \(Z\): the multiplier is known, while the residual has conditional mean zero. This does not say that residual and observation are independent. Their covariance vanishes, but the residual's conditional variance can still vary substantially across classes.

For example, let \(Z\) be equally likely one or two, and let an independent sign \(H\) be equally likely minus one or one. Set \(X=ZH\). Then \(E[X\mid Z]=0\), so the residual equals \(X\). It is uncorrelated with every function of \(Z\), yet its magnitude equals \(Z\). A predictor of the conditional mean cannot detect this dependence in conditional scale. For insurance decisions, a constant conditional mean is therefore compatible with different uncertainty and tail exposure across observable groups.

Finally, conditional expectation can exist when conditional variance is infinite. Finite first absolute moment suffices for the mean theory, while variance decompositions require square integrability for finite numerical statements. The information-based view preserves these boundaries and provides a reliable route to mixture and aggregate models.

12.6 Exercises with worked solutions

Compute each conditional quantity before averaging it. In particular, a conditional mean must remain a function of the conditioning variable until its distribution is used. For variance, display both the average conditional variance and the variance of conditional means; this makes any omitted source of uncertainty visible.

Exercise 1. Classes have probabilities 0.7 and 0.3, conditional means 2 and 6, and conditional variances 1 and 9. Find the unconditional mean and variance.

The mean is \(0.7(2)+0.3(6)=3.2\). Within-class variance is \(0.7(1)+0.3(9)=3.4\). Between-class variance is \(0.7(2-3.2)^2+0.3(6-3.2)^2=3.36\). Total variance is \(6.76\).

Exercise 2. Let Z be uniform on (0,1), and X conditional on Z=z be Bernoulli(z). Find E[X] and Var(X) by conditioning.

The conditional mean is \(z\) and variance \(z(1-z)\). Thus \(E[X]=1/2\), average conditional variance is \(1/2-1/3=1/6\), and variance of the conditional mean is \(1/12\). The total is \(1/4\), agreeing with an unconditional Bernoulli variable of probability \(1/2\).

Exercise 3. In the two-class claim example, quantify the extra squared error of always predicting 0.2.

The conditional-mean predictor has error 0.12. Its difference from 0.2 has mean square \(0.8(0.1-0.2)^2+0.2(0.4-0.2)^2=0.016\). Total prediction error is 0.136. Relative to the best constant 0.16, the excess is \((0.2-0.16)^2=0.0016\).

Exercise 4. If E[X|Z] is constant, must X and Z be independent?

No. Let \(Z\) be equally likely 1 or 2, and conditional on \(Z=z\), let \(X\) equal \(z\) or \(-z\) with equal probabilities. Every conditional mean is zero, but \(|X|=Z\), so dependence is exact. Conditional variance is \(Z^2\). Its expectation is \(5/2\), which is the unconditional variance.

Exercise 5. For square-integrable X,Y, derive total covariance conditional on Z.

Set \(M_X=E[X\mid Z]\), \(M_Y=E[Y\mid Z]\). Decompose each centered variable into a zero-conditional-mean residual plus its centered conditional mean. The two cross terms vanish by conditioning, leaving \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y\mid Z)]+\operatorname{Cov}(M_X,M_Y)\). Conditional independence removes the first term but generally leaves the second.

12.7 Exact decomposition in a finite model

The program expands the risk-class model into its full joint table and verifies total expectation, total variance, and the prediction-error decomposition using exact fractions.

"""Exact total moments and conditional prediction in two risk classes."""
from fractions import Fraction as F


classes = [(F(4, 5), F(1, 10)), (F(1, 5), F(2, 5))]
joint = [(weight*(p if x else 1-p), x, p)
         for weight, p in classes for x in (0, 1)]
mean = sum(prob*x for prob, x, p in joint)
variance = sum(prob*(x-mean)**2 for prob, x, p in joint)
within = sum(weight*p*(1-p) for weight, p in classes)
between = sum(weight*(p-mean)**2 for weight, p in classes)
prediction_error = sum(prob*(x-p)**2 for prob, x, p in joint)
constant_error = sum(prob*(x-F(1, 5))**2 for prob, x, p in joint)
extra = sum(weight*(p-F(1, 5))**2 for weight, p in classes)
assert sum(prob for prob, x, p in joint) == 1
assert variance == within + between
assert prediction_error == within
assert constant_error == within + extra
print(f"mean = {float(mean):.4f}; variance = {float(variance):.4f}")
print(f"within = {float(within):.4f}; between = {float(between):.4f}")
print(f"class prediction error = {float(prediction_error):.4f}")
print(f"constant 0.2 prediction error = {float(constant_error):.4f}")

mean = 0.1600; variance = 0.1344
within = 0.1200; between = 0.0144
class prediction error = 0.1200
constant 0.2 prediction error = 0.1360
