1.1 What is the experiment?

An audit selects four contracts from a portfolio of twenty. Eight contracts belong to a designated high-risk class. What is the probability that exactly two selected contracts belong to that class? Before counting anything, we must say what is random. The portfolio is fixed. The random outcome is the selected subset of four contracts. Confusing these objects produces a denominator of twenty where the correct denominator counts possible audits.

A random experiment is a specified procedure whose outcome is uncertain before observation. Its sample space, written \(\Omega\), contains the possible outcomes at the chosen level of description. If we select one contract uniformly, \(\Omega\) can be the twenty contract labels. If we select four without replacement and ignore order, \(\Omega\) consists of all four-element subsets. A portfolio becomes a sample space only after specifying a procedure such as selecting one member.

An event is a set of outcomes to which the probability model assigns a probability. In a finite sample space we may take every subset as an event. In general, an event collection \(\mathcal F\) must contain \(\Omega\), contain complements, and contain countable unions. Such a collection is called a sigma-algebra. These closure conditions make ordinary logical combinations legitimate events. We will not need the subtleties of nonmeasurable subsets, but the distinction prevents the false claim that every subset is always an event.

For a randomly selected contract, let \(A\) mean membership in a designated risk class and \(B\) mean a claim in a specified past period. Then \(A\cap B\) means both, \(A\cup B\) means at least one, and \(A^c\) means not \(A\). Probability uses inclusive “or.” Exactly one of the two events is \((A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B)\). Membership in a class alone supplies no empirical conclusion about actual claim frequency.

1.2 Probability as a consistent assignment

A probability measure \(P\) assigns a real number to each event. Its axioms are nonnegativity, \(P(\Omega)=1\), and countable additivity: whenever \(E_1,E_2,\ldots\) are pairwise disjoint events,

\[P\!\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}E_j\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}P(E_j).\]

Pairwise disjoint means \(E_i\cap E_j=\varnothing\) whenever \(i\ne j\). Applying additivity to \(\Omega\) and the empty set gives \(1=1+P(\varnothing)\), so the empty event has probability zero. Since \(E\) and \(E^c\) partition \(\Omega\), additivity gives \(P(E^c)=1-P(E)\). Nonnegativity of the complement then proves \(P(E)\le1\). The upper bound is a consequence of the axioms.

Set identities translate directly into probability arguments. If \(A\subseteq B\), then \(B=A\cup(B\setminus A)\) is a disjoint union. Consequently \(P(B)=P(A)+P(B\setminus A)\ge P(A)\). This monotonicity provides a useful diagnostic: a calculated probability for “large claim and coastal location” cannot exceed the probability of either event individually.

For overlapping events, decompose the union into \(A\setminus B\), \(A\cap B\), and \(B\setminus A\). Each component appears once in the union, but the intersection appears twice in \(P(A)+P(B)\). Therefore

\[P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).\]

For the illustrative values \(P(A)=0.10\), \(P(B)=0.20\), and \(P(A\cap B)=0.05\), the union has probability \(0.25\). The event that neither occurs is \((A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c\), so its probability is \(0.75\). The four disjoint regions \(A\cap B\), \(A\setminus B\), \(B\setminus A\), and \(A^c\cap B^c\) have probabilities \(0.05,0.05,0.15,0.75\), respectively. Their sum of one checks the entire specification.

For any finite collection, repeated addition also yields the union bound \(P(\bigcup_j E_j)\le\sum_jP(E_j)\). One proof replaces each event by the part not already covered by earlier events. These new parts are disjoint and each lies inside its original event. The bound requires no independence. It can exceed one, in which case replacing it by one improves the bound.

1.3 Counting equally likely outcomes

Suppose \(\Omega\) has \(m\) outcomes, each with probability \(1/m\). Finite additivity gives \(P(E)=|E|/m\), where \(|E|\) denotes the number of members of \(E\). This is a theorem about a uniform model, not a definition valid for every finite experiment. A loaded die still has six faces, but counting faces alone does not determine its probabilities.

If a procedure has \(k\) stages and every partial outcome at stage \(j-1\) has exactly \(n_j\) allowed continuations, its outcome count is \(n_1\cdots n_k\). The proof follows a branching tree: each existing branch acquires the same number of children. The equal-continuation condition matters. If one first choice allows two continuations and another allows five, the count is \(2+5\), not an arbitrary product. This counting rule makes no assertion about stochastic independence.

To assign three distinct audit roles to three different people among ten, there are ten choices for the first role, nine for the second, and eight for the third. Hence there are \(10\cdot9\cdot8=720\) assignments. For \(r\) ordered selections from \(n\) distinct objects without replacement, the same argument gives

\[(n)_r=n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!},\qquad 0\le r\le n.\]

The factorial \(n!\) is the product of the positive integers through \(n\), with \(0!=1\). If only the selected team matters, each unordered team has \(r!\) ordered representations. Dividing by this common multiplicity gives \(\binom nr=n!/[r!(n-r)!]\). Thus ten people form \(\binom{10}{3}=120\) three-person teams. Assigning secretary, treasurer, and chair would restore the factor \(3!\), because these roles distinguish outcomes.

1.4 The audit calculation and its boundaries

Return to the audit of twenty contracts, eight designated high-risk and twelve otherwise. Assume every four-element subset is selected with the same probability. There are \(\binom{20}{4}=4845\) possible audits. Exactly two high-risk contracts require choosing two of the eight and two of the twelve. Every favorable audit has one unique pair of these choices, so multiplication gives \(\binom82\binom{12}{2}=28\cdot66=1848\) favorable audits. Therefore

\[P(\text{exactly two})=\frac{1848}{4845}=\frac{616}{1615}\approx0.381424.\]

The general result for a population of \(M\) objects containing \(K\) marked objects and a uniform sample of size \(r\) is \(\binom Kk\binom{M-K}{r-k}/\binom Mr\). The possible marked counts satisfy \(\max(0,r-M+K)\le k\le\min(r,K)\). These bounds prevent selecting more marked or unmarked objects than exist. Summing the numerator over this range gives \(\binom Mr\), because it partitions all samples by their marked count.

Sampling without replacement and sampling with replacement have different outcome spaces. With replacement, an ordered sample of four labels has \(20^4\) possibilities; duplicates become possible. Without replacement, ordered samples have \((20)_4\) possibilities. Ignoring order is safe only when each unordered outcome has the same multiplicity. Four distinct labels have \(4!\) orders, whereas repeated labels have fewer. Mixing these denominators silently changes the model.

The original program enumerates all unordered audits and compares the result with the counting formula using exact rational arithmetic. It checks this finite example, including the complement “at least one marked contract.” It does not establish that an operational audit procedure is uniform. That assumption concerns how selection is implemented.

"""Exact enumeration of a uniform audit without replacement."""
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations
from math import comb

audits = list(combinations(range(20), 4))
exactly_two = sum(sum(label < 8 for label in a) == 2 for a in audits)
none_marked = sum(all(label >= 8 for label in a) for a in audits)
probability = Fraction(exactly_two, len(audits))
assert len(audits) == comb(20, 4)
assert exactly_two == comb(8, 2)*comb(12, 2)
assert none_marked == comb(12, 4)
assert probability == Fraction(616, 1615)
print(f"Audits: {len(audits)}; exactly two marked: {exactly_two}")
print(f"P(exactly two) = {probability} = {float(probability):.6f}")
print(f"P(at least one) = {1 - Fraction(none_marked, len(audits))}")

1.5 Three events and nonuniform selection

Adding a third event reveals the pattern behind inclusion-exclusion. In the sum \(P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\), an outcome in exactly two events is counted twice, while an outcome in all three is counted three times. Subtract the three pairwise intersection probabilities. This corrects each two-event outcome to one count, but subtracts a triple-event outcome three times, leaving it with zero counts. Restoring the triple intersection once yields

\[P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C)-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)+P(A\cap B\cap C).\]

This is a pointwise counting proof: every outcome in the union receives coefficient one and every outcome outside receives coefficient zero. Multiplying these coefficients by their probabilities and summing proves the formula, whether or not outcomes are equally likely. For three events each of probability 0.2, with pair intersections each 0.05 and triple intersection 0.01, the union probability is \(0.6-0.15+0.01=0.46\). The union bound 0.6 is valid but less informative because it ignores the specified overlaps.

Counting remains useful in a nonuniform experiment, but counts must be weighted. Suppose an audit chooses one of three files with probabilities \(1/2,1/3,1/6\). If the first and third files satisfy event \(E\), then \(|E|/|\Omega|=2/3\) happens to agree with the true probability \(1/2+1/6=2/3\). For an event containing the first two files, the same ratio remains \(2/3\), while its true probability is \(5/6\). An accidental agreement for one event does not validate a uniform model. The selection mechanism, rather than the event label or file count, determines the weights.

1.6 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Can P(A)=0.3, P(B)=0.4, and P(A intersect B)=0.35 coexist? Prove your answer.

Suppose \(P(A)=0.3\), \(P(B)=0.4\), and \(P(A\cap B)=0.35\). Show that no probability model has these values. Since \(A\cap B\subseteq A\), monotonicity requires \(P(A\cap B)\le0.3\). Equivalently the region \(A\setminus B\) would have probability \(0.3-0.35=-0.05\), violating nonnegativity.

Exercise 2. For P(A)=0.10, P(B)=0.20, and P(A intersect B)=0.05, find the probability of exactly one event.

For the chapter’s \(0.10,0.20,0.05\) example, find the probability of exactly one event. Its two disjoint regions have probabilities \(0.10-0.05\) and \(0.20-0.05\). Their sum is \(0.20\). The union probability \(0.25\) is larger by the intersection probability \(0.05\).

Exercise 3. Six people supply a two-person team. Count unassigned teams and assignments to reviewer and verifier.

Six people supply a two-person team. Count the teams and then the assignments to reviewer and verifier. The unordered count is \(\binom62=15\). Each team admits two role assignments, giving \(30=6\cdot5\). Dividing an ordered count by two is justified here because every team contains two distinct people.

Exercise 4. In a uniform four-contract audit from twenty contracts, eight marked, derive the probability of at least one marked selection.

In the twenty-contract audit, derive the probability of at least one high-risk selection. The complement selects all four from the twelve other contracts. Hence the answer is \(1-\binom{12}{4}/\binom{20}{4}=1-495/4845=290/323\). This avoids separately counting one, two, three, and four marked selections.

Exercise 5. An experiment reports no claim or at least one claim. Must both probabilities equal one-half?

A random experiment reports either “no claim” or “at least one claim.” Must each outcome have probability one-half? No. For any \(q\in[0,1]\), assigning probabilities \(1-q\) and \(q\) satisfies the axioms. The number of labels supplies no symmetry or selection mechanism. A uniform assignment needs an additional assumption.
