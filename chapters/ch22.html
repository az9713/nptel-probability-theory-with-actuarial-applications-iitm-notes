The final three recordings work through a thirty-question mock examination. The exercise is to identify the random object, choose a justified calculation, and notice the assumption or endpoint that can change the answer.

This chapter independently restates the mathematical problems and expands their solutions. It is a study reconstruction from the complete machine transcripts, not an official examination paper or a visual transcription of the slides. Spoken notation is corrected when normalization, dimensions, or the subsequent arithmetic resolves it. The source notes below identify the material ambiguities.

A disciplined solution method

Before doing algebra, decide whether the target is an event probability, a distribution, an expectation, a variance, or an approximation. Write the target in symbols. Then list the support and the assumptions that make the proposed tool applicable. For a density, normalization comes before any probability calculation. For a random sum, independence of the count and the severity sequence belongs beside the formula. For a normal approximation, centering and scaling are separate from the continuity correction.

The solutions below use \(\Phi(z)=\Pr(Z\le z)\) for a standard normal random variable \(Z\). Normal distributions are parametrized by mean and variance. Gamma distributions use shape and rate. A loss \(X\) is measured before policy modifications; a payment \(Y\) is the insurer's amount per ground-up loss, including zero payments. The synthetic models describe an examination calculation, rather than observed insurance experience.

The three source recordings suggest a ninety-minute practice session. That is a study exercise, not a statement about the current format of any professional examination. Attempt the thirty questions with the solutions closed. In a second pass, classify each mistake as a model error, an algebra error, a missing hypothesis, or an endpoint error. Those categories call for different repairs.

Questions 1–10: recognizing the mathematical object

Week 12 Lesson 3 · first mock block, from 02:28

Exercise 1. A portfolio is 80% low risk and 20% high risk. Annual claim probabilities within those groups are 0.1 and 0.3. Given a claim, find the probability of high-risk membership.

Let \(H\), \(L\), and \(C\) denote high-risk membership, low-risk membership, and a claim. Assume \(H,L\) partition the portfolio. The joint claim masses are \(\Pr(C\cap L)=0.8(0.1)=0.08\) and \(\Pr(C\cap H)=0.2(0.3)=0.06\). Thus \(\Pr(C)=0.14\), and Bayes gives \(\Pr(H\mid C)=0.06/0.14=3/7\). The conditional likelihood \(\Pr(C\mid H)=0.3\) is a different probability.

Exercise 2. Normalize \(f(x)=k(1+x)^{-3}\) for \(x>0\), with density zero elsewhere.

A density must integrate to one. Here \(\int_0^\infty(1+x)^{-3}\,dx=[-(1+x)^{-2}/2]_0^\infty=1/2\). Hence \(k=2\). Nonnegativity is then satisfied throughout the support. Normalization alone would not repair a proposed density that took negative values.

Exercise 3. Ten independent policies each generate a claim with probability 0.2. Find the probability that at least two generate claims.

The number \(N\) of policies with claims is binomial with parameters \(10,0.2\). The complement of \(\{N\ge2\}\) is \(\{N=0\}\cup\{N=1\}\), a disjoint union. Therefore \(\Pr(N\ge2)=1-0.8^{10}-10(0.2)0.8^9\). This is exact. The stated independence and common probability justify the binomial law; an expected count of two alone would not justify it.

Exercise 4. Binary random variables have \(\Pr(X=1)=0.7\), \(\Pr(Y=1)=0.6\), and \(\Pr(X=1,Y=1)=0.4\). Find their covariance.

Because \(XY=1\) exactly when both binary variables equal one, \(\mathbb E[XY]=0.4\). Also \(\mathbb E[X]=0.7\) and \(\mathbb E[Y]=0.6\). Thus \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0.4-0.7(0.6)=-0.02\). The complete table has masses \(0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4\) at \((0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\); all are nonnegative and sum to one. This checks that the specified moments describe a valid joint law.

Exercise 5. Independent component lifetimes are exponential with means 5 and 10. A series system fails when either component fails. Find its mean lifetime.

The system lifetime is \(T=\min(T_1,T_2)\). For \(t\ge0\), independence gives \(\Pr(T>t)=e^{-t/5}e^{-t/10}=e^{-0.3t}\). Thus the minimum is exponential with rate \(0.3\), so \(\mathbb E[T]=1/0.3=10/3\). The rates add; the means do not. Independence was used in multiplying survival probabilities.

Exercise 6. Given \(\Lambda=\lambda\), a count \(N\) is Poisson with mean \(\lambda\). The mixing variable \(\Lambda\) is uniform on \((0,6)\). Find \(\mathbb E[N]\).

The conditional mean is \(\mathbb E[N\mid\Lambda]=\Lambda\). Taking expectations gives \(\mathbb E[N]=\mathbb E[\Lambda]=3\). This is a mixed Poisson count. It is generally not an ordinary Poisson count of rate three: total variance gives \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=\mathbb E[\Lambda]+\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda)=3+3\), showing the extra variation produced by mixing.

Exercise 7. A random variable has MGF \(M_X(t)=[0.8/(0.8-t)]^4\) for \(t<0.8\). Find its variance.

This is the gamma MGF with shape \(\alpha=4\) and rate \(\beta=0.8\). For completeness, its cumulant generating function is \(K(t)=-4\log(1-t/0.8)\). Its second derivative at zero is \(K''(0)=4/0.8^2=6.25\), which equals the variance. The parameter 0.8 is a rate rather than a scale; interchanging conventions changes the answer.

Exercise 8. A variable has mean 100 and variance 64. Give a Chebyshev lower bound for \(\Pr(84<X<116)\).

The interval is \(|X-100|<16\). Chebyshev bounds its complement by \(\Pr(|X-100|\ge16)\le64/16^2=1/4\). Therefore the desired probability is at least \(3/4\). This is a lower bound, not a normal approximation or an exact probability. Using the complement also makes the strict versus non-strict endpoint convention explicit.

Exercise 9. Independent, identically distributed variables have mean 10 and variance 9. Use the uncorrected CLT approximation for \(\Pr(\sum_{i=1}^{100}X_i>1030)\).

The sum has mean 1,000 and variance 900, so its standard deviation is 30. The standardized threshold is \((1030-1000)/30=1\). The uncorrected normal approximation is \(1-\Phi(1)\). This finite-sample estimate is not guaranteed by the CLT alone. The moments do not determine whether the variables are continuous; a known lattice structure would need separate attention when choosing a continuity correction. Unspecified distribution type cannot be assumed continuous without loss of generality.

Exercise 10. An exponential ground-up loss has mean 500. An ordinary deductible is 200. Find the expected payment per ground-up loss.

The payment is \(Y=(X-200)_+\). Its mean is the tail integral \(\int_{200}^{\infty}\Pr(X>x)\,dx=\int_{200}^{\infty}e^{-x/500}\,dx=500e^{-0.4}\). This expectation includes losses that generate no payment. Conditional on a positive payment, memorylessness instead gives \(\mathbb E[Y\mid Y>0]=500\).

Questions 11–20: support, assumptions, and endpoints

Week 12 Lesson 4 · second mock block, from 01:18

Exercise 11. A group contains six members of category A and four of category B. A five-person committee is sampled uniformly without replacement. Find the probability of exactly three A members.

Every five-person subset is an equally likely outcome. There are \(\binom{10}{5}\) such subsets, while \(\binom63\binom42\) contain three A members and two B members. The probability is their ratio, \(10/21\). Order does not matter for a committee. Treating these draws as independent Bernoulli trials would use a different sampling mechanism.

Exercise 12. A variable has density \(3x^2\) on \((0,1)\). Find \(\mathbb E[1/X]\).

Use expectation under the original density: \(\mathbb E[1/X]=\int_0^1x^{-1}(3x^2)\,dx=3\int_0^1x\,dx=3/2\). The apparent singularity is integrable because the density vanishes rapidly enough near zero. Excluding the endpoint alone does not guarantee integrability; for a uniform variable on \((0,1)\), the analogous expectation is infinite.

Exercise 13. If \(X\sim N(40,16)\), find \(\Pr(X<48)\).

The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{16}=4\). Standardization gives \(\Pr(X<48)=\Phi((48-40)/4)=\Phi(2)\). The probability is exact under the assumed normal model, apart from numerical evaluation of \(\Phi\). No continuity correction applies to this continuous normal variable.

Exercise 14. Two variables have variances 25 and 16 and correlation 0.5. Find the variance of their sum.

Their standard deviations are 5 and 4. Their covariance is \(0.5(5)(4)=10\). Expanding the centered square gives \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=25+16+2(10)=61\). A correlation multiplies standard deviations, not variances. The covariance term cannot be dropped here.

Exercise 15. If \(U\) is uniform on \((0,1)\), find the density of \(T=-\log U\).

The transformed support is \(t>0\). For \(t\ge0\), the decreasing transformation reverses the inequality: \(\Pr(-\log U\le t)=\Pr(U\ge e^{-t})=1-e^{-t}\). Differentiating gives \(f_T(t)=e^{-t}\) for \(t>0\), zero elsewhere. This is the exponential distribution of rate one, and it explains an inverse-transform simulation rule.

Exercise 16. A count has mean 10. An iid severity sequence, independent of that count, has mean 500 per severity. Find the mean random sum.

For \(S=\sum_{i=1}^NY_i\), conditional on \(N=n\), the expected total is \(n(500)\). Taking expectations gives \(\mathbb E[S]=500\mathbb E[N]=5000\). The empty sum is zero. The stated independence ensures the conditional severity means remain 500 when the count is known.

Exercise 17. Independent gamma variables have shapes 2 and 3, both with rate one. Find the distribution and expectation of their sum.

The product MGF is \((1-t)^{-2}(1-t)^{-3}=(1-t)^{-5}\) near zero. MGF uniqueness identifies a gamma distribution of shape five and rate one, with expectation five. Linearity alone gives the expectation even without independence, but the gamma distributional conclusion uses independence and a common rate.

Exercise 18. A nonnegative loss has mean 100. Give a Markov upper bound for the probability that it is at least 400.

Pointwise, \(X\ge400\mathbf1_{\{X\ge400\}}\). Taking expectations yields \(\Pr(X\ge400)\le100/400=1/4\). Nonnegativity is required; the mean alone does not control a positive tail for a signed variable. This is a bound rather than an estimate of the actual tail.

Exercise 19. A fair coin is tossed 400 times independently. Approximate the probability of at least 215 heads using continuity correction.

The head count has mean 200 and variance 100. The event \(H\ge215\) is approximated by a normal variable exceeding 214.5, so the standardized threshold is 1.45. The answer is approximately \(1-\Phi(1.45)\). The source uses interpolation between rounded table entries and obtains about 0.0738; direct evaluation of the normal CDF produces the slightly different value printed below. Both are approximations to the binomial probability, with table interpolation introducing an additional numerical approximation.

Exercise 20. A loss is uniform on \((0,100)\). A policy pays \(Y=\min(X,40)\). Find \(\mathbb E[Y]\).

For \(0<y<40\), \(\Pr(Y>y)=\Pr(X>y)=1-y/100\). Thus \(\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^{40}(1-y/100)\,dy=40-40^2/200=32\). The payment has a mass of 0.6 at the cap 40. Integrating only the continuous part of its distribution would omit that mass.

Questions 21–30: joint laws and insurance layers

Week 12 Lesson 5 · final mock block, from 01:13

Exercise 21. Three disjoint portfolio groups have proportions 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 and claim probabilities 0.05, 0.1, 0.2 respectively. Find the overall claim probability.

The groups partition the portfolio and their proportions sum to one. Total probability gives \(0.5(0.05)+0.3(0.1)+0.2(0.2)=0.095\). A weighted average must lie between the smallest and largest conditional probabilities, as this result does. The machine transcript briefly gives an inconsistent third proportion; the normalized weights and the following arithmetic support 0.2, which is the explicit assumption used here.

Exercise 22. A CDF equals \((x^2-1)/8\) for \(1<x<3\), zero at and below one, and one at and above three. Find \(\Pr(X>2)\).

The CDF definition includes the point at the threshold: \(F_X(2)=\Pr(X\le2)=3/8\). Its complement is \(\Pr(X>2)=1-F_X(2)=5/8\). The full CDF specified here is increasing and continuous at both support endpoints. For a distribution with an atom at two, the event \(X\ge2\) would instead require the left limit of the CDF.

Exercise 23. A homogeneous Poisson process has rate six arrivals per hour. Find the probability of exactly two arrivals in twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes is one third of an hour, so the count over that interval is Poisson with parameter \(6/3=2\). Consequently its probability of two arrivals is \(e^{-2}2^2/2!=2e^{-2}\). Rate and interval duration must use compatible time units. The Poisson parameter is the expected count over the specified interval, not the hourly rate used without rescaling.

Exercise 24. A joint density is \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)=2(x+y)\) for \(0<x<y<1\), zero elsewhere. Find \(\Pr(X<1/2)\).

For a fixed \(x\in(0,1)\), the allowed \(y\)-values run from \(x\) to one. The marginal density is \(\int_x^12(x+y)\,dy=1+2x-3x^2\). Its integral from zero to one equals one, checking normalization. The required probability is \(\int_0^{1/2}(1+2x-3x^2)\,dx=[x+x^2-x^3]_0^{1/2}=5/8\). The triangular support determines the integration limits. The factor 2 multiplies the entire parenthesis; the alternative \(2x+y\) would fail normalization.

Exercise 25. Two independent variables have CDF \(F(x)=x^3\) on \((0,1)\). Find the density of their maximum.

Let \(M=\max(X_1,X_2)\). Both variables must be at most \(m\) for the maximum to be at most \(m\). Independence gives \(F_M(m)=m^3m^3=m^6\) on \((0,1)\). Differentiation yields \(f_M(m)=6m^5\), zero outside that interval. Its integral is one. The CDF \(m^6\) and the density \(6m^5\) answer different questions.

Exercise 26. Given \(\Lambda\), a count is Poisson with parameter \(\Lambda\). Suppose \(\mathbb E[\Lambda]=2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda)=1\). Find the count variance.

Conditional mean and variance both equal \(\Lambda\). Total variance therefore gives \(\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(N\mid\Lambda)]+\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[N\mid\Lambda])=2+1=3\). Its unconditional mean is two, so the count variance exceeds its mean. Replacing the mixture by \(\operatorname{Poisson}(2)\) preserves the mean and loses the heterogeneity variance.

Exercise 27. Independent variables have laws \(N(10,4)\) and \(N(20,5)\). Find the probability that their sum exceeds 33.

The sum is exactly normal with mean 30 and variance nine. Its standard deviation is three, so the probability is \(1-\Phi((33-30)/3)=1-\Phi(1)\). This is exact under the given independent normal model. It has the same standardized expression as Question 9, where the normal law was an approximation rather than a distributional assumption.

Exercise 28. If \(N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(100)\), approximate \(\Pr(N\le90)\) with continuity correction.

Approximate the count by a normal variable of mean 100 and standard deviation ten. The inclusive upper boundary at 90 becomes 90.5. The answer is approximately \(\Phi((90.5-100)/10)=\Phi(-0.95)\). A direct CDF evaluation is printed below; the spoken decimal in the source is slightly inconsistent with its own rounded table values. The standardized expression specifies the approximation unambiguously.

Exercise 29. An exponential loss has mean 100. A contract pays the layer between ground-up loss levels 50 and 200. Find its mean payment per ground-up loss.

The deductible is \(d=50\), the ground-up ceiling is \(L=200\), and the layer width is \(u=L-d=150\). Thus \(Y=\min((X-50)_+,150)\). Equivalently, \(Y=\min(X,200)-\min(X,50)\). Integrating the exponential survival function over the covered layer gives \(\mathbb E[Y]=\int_{50}^{200}e^{-x/100}\,dx=100(e^{-0.5}-e^{-2})\). Reading 200 as the payment cap would describe a different contract.

Exercise 30. A Poisson claim count has mean five. An iid payment sequence, independent of that count, has first moment 50 and second raw moment 4,000. Find the aggregate payment variance.

Conditional on the count, aggregate variance is \(N\operatorname{Var}(Y)\), and the conditional mean is \(N\mathbb E[Y]\). Total variance gives \(\operatorname{Var}(S)=\mathbb E[N]\operatorname{Var}(Y)+\operatorname{Var}(N)(\mathbb E[Y])^2\). For a Poisson count both count moments equal five, so the terms combine to \(5\mathbb E[Y^2]=20{,}000\). The raw second moment is required; multiplying the severity variance alone would omit count uncertainty.
