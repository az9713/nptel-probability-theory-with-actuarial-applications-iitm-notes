7.1 Marginal losses do not specify a pair

Two claim indicators each have probability one-half of equaling one. Does that determine the probability that both equal one? No. They might always agree, always differ, or behave independently. Their separate distributions are identical in all three constructions, while their simultaneous behavior changes. A model for several quantities requires a joint law.

Let \(X,Y\) be real random variables defined on the same probability space. Their joint distribution assigns probabilities to events involving the pair \((X,Y)\). The joint cumulative distribution function is \(F_{X,Y}(x,y)=P(X\le x,Y\le y)\). The notation inside the probability denotes intersection. The marginal distributions describe each variable separately and are recovered by ignoring the other coordinate.

For finite \(a\lt b\) and \(c\lt d\), inclusion-exclusion applied to the two coordinates gives the rectangle probability

\[P(a\lt X\le b,c\lt Y\le d)=F_{X,Y}(b,d)-F_{X,Y}(a,d)-F_{X,Y}(b,c)+F_{X,Y}(a,c).\]

Every genuine joint CDF makes this expression nonnegative. Increasing in each coordinate alone does not impose that full condition. Joint distributions encode how probability occupies a plane, not just how it accumulates along either axis.

7.2 A joint probability table and its margins

For a discrete pair, the joint probability mass function is \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y)\). Its nonnegative entries sum to one over the pair’s countable support. To find \(P(X=x)\), take the disjoint union over all possible \(Y\) values, obtaining \(p_X(x)=\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)\). Likewise \(p_Y(y)=\sum_x p_{X,Y}(x,y)\). Summation is over allowed pairs, with mass zero outside the support.

The first marginal is Bernoulli with probability 0.5; the second is Bernoulli with probability 0.4. Their intersection probability \(P(X=1,Y=1)=0.3\) is a table entry, whereas \(P(X=1\text{ or }Y=1)=0.5+0.4-0.3=0.6\). The distribution of the total \(X+Y\) is found by summing along equal-total cells: probabilities are 0.4 at zero, 0.3 at one, and 0.3 at two.

For any measurable function \(g\), an integrable discrete expectation satisfies \(E[g(X,Y)]=\sum_x\sum_y g(x,y)p_{X,Y}(x,y)\). The proof groups underlying outcomes by their pair values, exactly as for a single variable. For this table, \(E[XY]=0.3\), because the product is one only at \((1,1)\). It differs from \(E[X]E[Y]=0.2\). Separate averages do not ordinarily determine the average of a product.

If \(p_Y(y)\gt0\), the conditional PMF is \(p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=p_{X,Y}(x,y)/p_Y(y)\). Summing over \(x\) gives one, so this is a probability law for each such \(y\). At \(Y=1\), the conditional probability of \(X=1\) is \(0.3/0.4=0.75\), larger than its marginal probability 0.5. At \(Y=0\), it is \(0.2/0.6=1/3\). Weighting these conditional probabilities by 0.4 and 0.6 recovers 0.5.

7.3 A joint density lives on a support region

A pair has a joint PDF if a nonnegative function \(f_{X,Y}\) satisfies \(P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\) for measurable plane regions \(A\). Its integral over the plane is one. Each point and each line have probability zero under such a law, because they have zero area. Not every pair of continuous marginal variables has a joint PDF: if \(Y=X\), the pair lies entirely on a line.

For an original example, put constant density two on the triangle \(0\lt x\lt y\lt1\), and zero elsewhere. The triangle’s area is one-half, so the total probability is one. To obtain the marginal of \(X\), fix \(x\) and integrate along the vertical slice \(x\lt y\lt1\). To obtain the marginal of \(Y\), fix \(y\) and integrate along the horizontal slice \(0\lt x\lt y\). Thus

\[f_X(x)=\int_x^1 2\,dy=2(1-x),\quad 0\lt x\lt1;\qquad f_Y(y)=\int_0^y2\,dx=2y,\quad0\lt y\lt1.\]

Both marginal densities are zero outside \((0,1)\). Their integrals each equal one. The limits depend on the support, even though the density formula is constant. Integrating from zero to one in both coordinates would describe a square and double the total probability.

For \(0\lt a\lt1\), the event \(X\gt a\) is the upper-right subtriangle with area \((1-a)^2/2\), so its probability is \((1-a)^2\). Marginal integration confirms \(\int_a^1 2(1-x)dx=(1-a)^2\). The event \(Y\le a\) instead has probability \(a^2\). These formulas reflect the ordered support: \(X\) is always below \(Y\), so the two marginal laws should not look identical.

Marginalization follows from nonnegative integration: \(P(X\in B)=\int_B[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y)dy]dx\). Hence the bracketed integral is a version of \(f_X\). The word “version” matters because changing density values on a zero-area set leaves probabilities unchanged. Integration statements, rather than isolated heights, define the law.

7.4 Conditioning when a point has probability zero

For a density-based \(Y\), \(P(Y=y)=0\), so dividing \(P(X\in B,Y=y)\) by \(P(Y=y)\) gives an undefined zero-over-zero expression. Instead, when \(0\lt f_Y(y)\lt\infty\), define a conditional density version by

\[f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}.\]

For almost every such \(y\), it integrates to one. More fundamentally it satisfies the reconstruction identity \(P(X\in B,Y\in C)=\int_C[\int_B f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)dx]f_Y(y)dy\). Substituting the ratio verifies this identity by cancellation and integration. Values on marginal-probability-zero sets can be changed arbitrarily without altering the identity. A conditional law is therefore determined only almost everywhere in the conditioning variable.

The ratio also has a shrinking-interval interpretation when the relevant densities are continuous at the conditioning value. For a small interval around \(y\), both the joint numerator integrated over that interval and the marginal denominator scale with its width. Their ratio tends to the density ratio after appropriate regularity permits the limit. This interpretation explains the formula, but the reconstruction identity is the definition that survives beyond pointwise continuity.

In the triangular example, for \(0\lt y\lt1\), the conditional density of \(X\) given \(Y=y\) is \(2/(2y)=1/y\) on \((0,y)\). Thus it is uniform on an interval whose length depends on the observed value. Its conditional mean is \(\int_0^y x/y\,dx=y/2\). Given \(X=x\), the density of \(Y\) is \(1/(1-x)\) on \((x,1)\). The two conditional laws have different supports and answer different questions.

No conditional density is obtained from this ratio at \(y=0\), where the marginal density formula is zero. Assigning any probability law at that exceptional conditioning value does not change the joint law. In applications, rounding or observing a measurement interval can supply a positive-probability conditioning event; that is a different, explicitly specified observation model.

7.5 Factorization and its failure

Random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent when \(P(X\in B,Y\in C)=P(X\in B)P(Y\in C)\) for every pair of measurable sets \(B,C\). In the discrete setting this is equivalent to \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\) for every pair of values. Necessity follows by taking singleton sets. Sufficiency follows by summing the factorization over arbitrary sets.

When a joint PDF exists, independence is equivalent to \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) almost everywhere. Factorization implies the defining rectangle probabilities because the two integrals separate. Conversely, independence makes the product density assign the same probabilities to all rectangles as the joint law; uniqueness of the associated probability measure and of densities up to null sets gives the almost-everywhere identity. Thus isolated discrepancies in density values are irrelevant, while discrepancies over a positive-area region are decisive.

The triangular law fails independence. For example, both events \(X\gt3/4\) and \(Y\lt1/4\) have probability \(1/16\), but their intersection is impossible because \(X\lt Y\). Their joint probability is zero rather than \(1/256\). Notice that the formula “two” contains no visible cross term; that does not create independence because its triangular support couples the variables.

The discrete table also fails factorization: \(p(1,1)=0.3\), whereas \(p_X(1)p_Y(1)=0.2\). The original program checks the marginals and conditional probability using exact fractions. Testing one failed cell disproves independence; testing one successful cell generally cannot prove it. You can now recover margins, identify conditional laws, and recognize dependence encoded by a support restriction.

"""Marginalization and conditioning on a finite joint law."""
from fractions import Fraction as F

joint = {(0, 0): F(4, 10), (0, 1): F(1, 10),
         (1, 0): F(2, 10), (1, 1): F(3, 10)}
px = {x: sum(p for (a, b), p in joint.items() if a == x)
      for x in (0, 1)}
py = {y: sum(p for (a, b), p in joint.items() if b == y)
      for y in (0, 1)}
assert sum(joint.values()) == 1
conditional = joint[1, 1]/py[1]
assert conditional == F(3, 4)
assert joint[1, 1] != px[1]*py[1]
print(f"P(X=1) = {px[1]}; P(Y=1) = {py[1]}")
print(f"P(X=1 | Y=1) = {conditional}")
print(f"Joint at (1,1) = {joint[1, 1]}; product = {px[1]*py[1]}")

P(X=1 | Y=1) = 3/4
Joint at (1,1) = 3/10; product = 1/5

7.6 A nonrectangular event requires a split

The triangular law also illustrates why the event region and the density support must both enter the limits. Find \(P(X+Y\le1)\) when density is two on \(0\lt x\lt y\lt1\). At fixed \(x\), the support requires \(y\gt x\), while the event requires \(y\le1-x\). These inequalities can coexist only for \(0\lt x\lt1/2\). Therefore the probability is \(\int_0^{1/2}\int_x^{1-x}2\,dy\,dx=\int_0^{1/2}(2-4x)dx=1/2\).

Reversing the order of integration requires two pieces. At fixed \(y\), the upper limit for \(x\) is the smaller of \(y\) and \(1-y\). The controlling bound changes at \(y=1/2\), giving \(\int_0^{1/2}\int_0^y2\,dx\,dy+\int_{1/2}^{1}\int_0^{1-y}2\,dx\,dy=1/4+1/4=1/2\). Agreement checks the geometric interpretation. Extending either inner upper limit across the whole interval would include points outside the event or outside the support.

A conditional calculation provides a third representation. Given \(Y=y\), the variable \(X\) is uniform on \((0,y)\). For \(y\le1/2\), every allowed \(X\) satisfies the event. For \(y\gt1/2\), the conditional probability is \((1-y)/y\). Weighting by marginal density \(2y\) reproduces the same two integrals. This connects conditional probabilities to joint-region integration and exposes the probability weighting that a plain average of conditional answers would miss.

7.7 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Using the table in Section 7.2, find P(Y=1 given X=0) and compare it with P(X=0 given Y=1).

Find \(P(Y=1\mid X=0)\) in the chapter’s table. Its numerator is 0.1 and denominator is the first row total 0.5, so the answer is 0.2. This differs from \(P(X=0\mid Y=1)=0.1/0.4=0.25\), because the conditioning populations differ.

Exercise 2. For density two on 0<x<y<1, find P(X<y/2 given Y=y), for 0<y<1.

For density two on \(0\lt x\lt y\lt1\), find \(P(X\lt y/2\mid Y=y)\) for \(0\lt y\lt1\). The conditional density is \(1/y\) on \((0,y)\), so integration over \((0,y/2)\) gives one-half. The result is constant despite the changing support length.

Exercise 3. Normalize f(x,y)=cxy on the unit square, find both margins, and test independence.

Let \(f(x,y)=cxy\) on \(0\lt x\lt1,0\lt y\lt1\). Its integral is \(c(1/2)(1/2)=c/4\), hence \(c=4\). The marginal densities are \(2x\) and \(2y\). Their product equals \(4xy\) on the full square and zero elsewhere, proving independence.

Exercise 4. Let X be uniform on (0,1) and Y=X. Prove continuous margins need not imply a joint PDF.

Let \(X\) be uniform on \((0,1)\), and put \(Y=X\). Both marginals are uniform. The diagonal \(\{(x,y):x=y\}\) has area zero but joint probability one, so no joint PDF can represent this law. Independence also fails: \(P(X\le1/2,Y\le1/2)=1/2\), not \(1/4\).

Exercise 5. Construct two Bernoulli-half variables that always differ and compare their joint law with independence.

Construct two Bernoulli-half variables that always differ. Assign probability one-half to \((0,1)\) and one-half to \((1,0)\), zero elsewhere. Each marginal has mass one-half at zero and one, while \(P(X=1,Y=1)=0\). Independent Bernoulli-half variables have the same margins but joint probability one-quarter at that cell.
