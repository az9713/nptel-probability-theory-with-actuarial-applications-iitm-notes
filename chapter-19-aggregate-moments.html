Random sums and aggregate-loss moments

19.1 Why random claim counts create another variance term

Two portfolios can have the same average claim count and the same severity distribution but different annual variability. A fixed count of ten claims is less uncertain than a count that is zero in half the years and twenty in the other half. Computing aggregate variance requires accounting for both the variability of individual payments and the variability of how many payments occur.

Let \(N\) be a nonnegative integer-valued random variable counting gross-loss events in one year. Let \(Y_1,Y_2,\ldots\) be independent, identically distributed nonnegative payments per gross loss. Assume that the entire payment sequence is independent of \(N\). Define

\[S=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_i,\]

where the empty sum for \(N=0\) is zero. The common payment mean is \(m=E[Y_1]\), and its variance is \(v=\operatorname{Var}(Y_1)\). Payment severities can contain zero because the count here includes gross-loss events below a deductible. An alternative count of positive payments requires a corresponding conditional severity law.

The independence from the whole sequence is substantive. It means that learning the number of events does not change the distribution of each payment. A catastrophe year may violate it because an unusually large number of losses can also have unusually large severities. Conditioning still works in that case, but the conditional moments need to be modeled explicitly.

19.2 Condition first, then average

Given \(N=n\), the aggregate is an ordinary sum of \(n\) independent payments. Independence of count and severities leaves their means and variances unchanged by this conditioning. Consequently

\[E[S\mid N=n]=nm,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S\mid N=n)=nv.\]

For nonnegative payments, the expectation identity holds even with infinite means, interpreted through nonnegative sums. If \(m=\infty\), the conditional mean is infinite for \(n>0\), while for \(n=0\) the empty sum has conditional mean zero. Do not interpret that empty sum as the undefined product zero times infinity. To work with a finite variance below, assume \(E[N^2]<\infty\) and \(E[Y_1^2]<\infty\). The tower property then gives

\[E[S]=E[E[S\mid N]]=mE[N].\]

The formula multiplies the expected event count by expected payment per event. Neither replacing \(N\) with its mean inside the random sum nor imagining a fractional number of claims is the proof. Conditional expectation provides the justification.

Original worked example. Suppose \(P(N=0)=P(N=2)=1/2\), and each payment independently equals 1 or 3 with equal probabilities. Here \(E[N]=1\), \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=1\), \(m=2\), and \(v=1\). Conditional aggregate means are 0 and 4, so the unconditional mean is 2. This agrees with \(mE[N]\), although neither a typical count nor a typical payment needs to equal its expectation.

19.3 Deriving the aggregate variance

The law of total variance separates fluctuations within a count from fluctuations between counts:

\[\operatorname{Var}(S)=E[\operatorname{Var}(S\mid N)]+\operatorname{Var}(E[S\mid N])=vE[N]+m^2\operatorname{Var}(N).\]

The first term is expected within-count severity variability. The second is count variability translated into monetary units by the squared mean payment. Both terms have units of money squared. Omitting the second would give zero aggregate variance whenever severity is deterministic, even though a random number of fixed payments is random.

The same result follows by computing a raw second moment. Conditional independence gives

\[E[S^2\mid N=n]=nE[Y_1^2]+n(n-1)m^2.\]

There are \(n\) diagonal terms \(Y_i^2\) and \(n(n-1)\) ordered cross terms \(Y_iY_j\). Their expectations factor to \(m^2\). Averaging and subtracting \((mE[N])^2\) yields the variance formula. This second derivation exposes where independence of distinct severities enters.

In the worked example, aggregate payments are 0, 2, 4, and 6 with probabilities \(1/2,1/8,1/4,1/8\). Thus \(E[S^2]=0+4/8+16/4+36/8=9\), and variance is \(9-2^2=5\). The conditional formula gives \(1(1)+2^2(1)=5\). A fixed one-claim model with the same mean count would instead have variance 1.

The dispersion index of a count with positive finite mean is \(D=\operatorname{Var}(N)/E[N]\). Aggregate variance can be written \(E[N](v+Dm^2)\). At a fixed mean frequency and severity law, larger count dispersion increases aggregate variance. This comparison does not determine tail probabilities, because two moments do not specify a full distribution.

19.4 The compound Poisson simplification

If \(N\) has a Poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda>0\), its mean and variance both equal \(\lambda\). Therefore

\[E[S]=\lambda m,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S)=\lambda(v+m^2)=\lambda E[Y_1^2].\]

The second raw severity moment, not severity variance alone, enters the last expression. The count's uncertainty supplies the missing \(\lambda m^2\). For deterministic severity \(Y_i=b\), aggregate payment is \(bN\), so its variance is \(b^2\lambda\), exactly as required.

Where the severity moment-generating function \(M_Y(t)=E[e^{tY}]\) is finite and the composition is defined, conditioning on \(N\) gives

\[M_S(t)=E[M_Y(t)^N]=\exp\{\lambda(M_Y(t)-1)\}.\]

The first equality uses independence to multiply conditional transforms. The second substitutes the Poisson probability mass function and sums its exponential series. Taking the logarithm shows that the \(k\)-th derivative at zero of \(\log M_S(t)\), called the \(k\)-th cumulant, is \(\lambda E[Y^k]\) when the required differentiation is justified. This compact identity explains the raw second moment in aggregate variance.

If \(Y>0\) almost surely, the aggregate has an atom \(P(S=0)=P(N=0)=e^{-\lambda}\). If zero severity occurs with probability \(1-q\), the atom is larger: \(P(S=0)=E[(1-q)^N]=e^{-\lambda q}\). A continuous normal approximation cannot reproduce that atom. Small effective payment frequency therefore demands particular care.

19.5 The count and severity bases must match

Suppose each gross-loss payment is positive with probability \(q>0\), independently across events. Write \(Y^P\) for its conditional positive-payment distribution, with mean \(m_P\), variance \(v_P\), and second raw moment \(r_P\). Count only positive payments and call that count \(K\). Given \(N=n\), the indicators of positive payment are independent Bernoulli variables, so \(K\mid N=n\) is binomial with parameters \(n,q\).

\[E[K]=qE[N],\qquad \operatorname{Var}(K)=q(1-q)E[N]+q^2\operatorname{Var}(N).\]

These follow by conditional mean and variance, just as for the aggregate. The selected positive amounts can be represented by independent copies of \(Y^P\) independent of \(K\). To see the factorization, condition on the indicator sequence: independence of the original severities makes the conditional positive values independent with the same law, regardless of the locations or number of positive indicators. Averaging over indicator patterns preserves this property.

The per-loss moments satisfy \(m=qm_P\) and \(v=qv_P+q(1-q)m_P^2\). Substituting these and the count identities proves

\[mE[N]=m_PE[K],\qquad vE[N]+m^2\operatorname{Var}(N)=v_PE[K]+m_P^2\operatorname{Var}(K).\]

For Poisson \(N\), the probability-generating function of \(K\) is \(E[z^K]=E[(1-q+qz)^N]=e^{\lambda q(z-1)}\). Thus \(K\) is Poisson with parameter \(\lambda q\). Both bases then give variance \(\lambda E[Y^2]=\lambda qE[(Y^P)^2]\). Combining the thinned count with unconditional severity would apply the factor \(q\) twice and underprice the portfolio.

19.6 What breaks without the assumptions?

Shared severity shocks can add a quadratic term. Suppose severities are exchangeable, independent of \(N\), with common variance \(v\) and covariance \(\gamma\) between distinct payments. Conditional variance is then \(nv+n(n-1)\gamma\). Total variance becomes

\[vE[N]+\gamma E[N(N-1)]+m^2\operatorname{Var}(N).\]

Even a small positive covariance can matter when the count is large, because the number of pairs grows quadratically. Setting \(\gamma=0\) recovers the independent-severity formula, but zero pairwise covariance alone would not justify transform factorization.

Count-severity dependence is different. Let \(N\) be 1 or 2 with equal probability, and let every severity equal \(N\). Each severity then has marginal mean 1.5, while \(E[N]=1.5\). Their product is 2.25. The aggregate is \(N^2\), with mean 2.5. The failure comes from using marginal severity moments after conditioning on the count. The remedy is to compute \(E[S\mid N]\) from the actual model rather than apply a memorized product.

19.7 A discrete compound law can be computed recursively

Exact aggregate probabilities are available for nonnegative integer payments under a compound Poisson model. Let \(f_j=P(Y=j)\), \(j=0,1,\ldots\), and \(g_k=P(S=k)\). Define probability-generating functions \(F(z)=\sum_jf_jz^j\) and \(G(z)=\sum_kg_kz^k\), for \(0\leq z\leq1\). Conditioning gives \(G(z)=\exp\{\lambda(F(z)-1)\}\).

At zero, \(g_0=\exp\{\lambda(f_0-1)\}\), which agrees with the thinning result. Differentiating inside the open unit disk gives \(G'(z)=\lambda F'(z)G(z)\). Matching the coefficient of \(z^{k-1}\) on both sides yields

\[g_k=\frac\lambda k\sum_{j=1}^k j f_jg_{k-j},\qquad k\geq1.\]

Every term on the right uses an aggregate probability with a smaller index. The recursion therefore starts from \(g_0\) and computes probabilities successively. The zero-severity probability appears in initialization, while positive severity masses determine subsequent increments. This separation prevents confusing a zero event payment with no gross event.

For an original example, suppose each claim pays 1 or 2 equally often and the Poisson intensity is 2. Then \(g_0=e^{-2}\). The recurrence gives \(g_1=e^{-2}\) and \(g_2=(3/2)e^{-2}\). The last probability has two contributions: one claim paying 2, and two claims each paying 1. Their probabilities are \(e^{-2}\) and \(e^{-2}/2\), respectively.

Recursion produces an exact model calculation up to numerical arithmetic and any cutoff used in computation. A finite cutoff must retain or bound omitted tail mass before reporting a complete distribution. Choosing a monetary unit discretizes a continuous severity law and introduces a separate approximation. The probability identities above are exact for an already specified integer severity model, not for an unspecified rounding convention.

19.8 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Separate the two sources of variance. A count has mean 8 and variance 20. Severity has mean 100 and variance 2500. Under the random-sum assumptions, find aggregate mean and variance.

The mean is \(8(100)=800\). Severity variability contributes \(8(2500)=20000\). Count variability contributes \(20(100^2)=200000\). Total variance is 220000, so count uncertainty supplies ten times the within-count component. A Poisson count with mean 8 would give variance 100000 instead.

Exercise 2. A deterministic payment check. If every severity equals b and N is binomial with parameters n,p, determine S and its moments.

Pathwise \(S=bN\). Hence \(E[S]=bnp\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S)=b^2np(1-p)\). The general formula gives the same result because severity variance is zero and count variance is \(np(1-p)\). This checks the count term without any severity uncertainty.

Exercise 3. Deductible thinning. Gross event count is Poisson with mean 12. Payment is positive with probability 0.4, and positive payments have mean 300 and second raw moment 180000. Compute moments using both bases.

Per-loss mean is 120 and second moment is 72000. The gross-count calculation gives mean 1440 and variance 864000. Positive-payment frequency is Poisson with mean 4.8. It gives mean \(4.8(300)=1440\) and variance \(4.8(180000)=864000\). Using 4.8 with the per-loss moments would instead understate both quantities by a factor of 0.4.

Exercise 4. Correlated claims. Let N=100 deterministically. Severity mean is 1, variance is 4, and each distinct pair has covariance 0.1. Find aggregate variance and explain the independence error.

There are \(100(99)\) ordered pairs, so aggregate variance is \(100(4)+100(99)(0.1)=1390\). Adding only individual variances gives 400 and misses 990. A valid example realizing positive covariance is \(Y_i=A+B_i\), with shared nonnegative shock \(A\) of variance 0.1 and independent nonnegative \(B_i\) of variance 3.9.

Exercise 5. Moment matching does not identify a count law. Compare a Poisson count of mean 1 with a count taking 0 and 2 equally often. Both use constant severity 1.

Both counts have mean 1 and variance 1, so both aggregates do as well. Yet zero aggregate probabilities are \(e^{-1}\) and 1/2. Exceedance probabilities above 2 are positive for Poisson and zero for the two-point count. The first two moments suffice for the moment formula, but not for tail pricing.

19.9 Enumerating a random sum

The original standard-library program enumerates the finite count-severity example and checks both moments by exact fractions. It also compares per-loss and per-payment Poisson calculations. Its assertions test the implemented finite models, including the zero-count state.

"""Original exact enumeration of a finite random sum."""
from collections import defaultdict
from fractions import Fraction as F
from itertools import product

counts = [(0, F(1, 2)), (2, F(1, 2))]
severities = [(1, F(1, 2)), (3, F(1, 2))]
aggregate = defaultdict(F)
for n, pn in counts:
    for outcomes in product(severities, repeat=n):
        amount, probability = 0, pn
        for value, p in outcomes:
            amount += value
            probability *= p
        aggregate[amount] += probability
assert sum(aggregate.values()) == 1
mean = sum(s * p for s, p in aggregate.items())
second = sum(s**2 * p for s, p in aggregate.items())
assert mean == 2
assert second - mean**2 == 5
print("Aggregate law: " + ", ".join(
    f"{s}: {p}" for s, p in sorted(aggregate.items())))
print(f"Aggregate mean, variance: {mean}, {second - mean**2}")
rate, q, mp, rp = F(12), F(2, 5), F(300), F(180000)
assert rate * (q * mp) == (rate * q) * mp == 1440
assert rate * (q * rp) == (rate * q) * rp == 864000
print("Both Poisson count bases: mean=1440, variance=864000")

Aggregate law: 0: 1/2, 2: 1/8, 4: 1/4, 6: 1/8
Aggregate mean, variance: 2, 5
Both Poisson count bases: mean=1440, variance=864000
