4.1 Which average answers the financial question?

A hypothetical loss is zero with probability 0.7, 100 with probability 0.2, and 500 with probability 0.1. A contract pays only the amount above an ordinary deductible of 100. What is its expected payment? Averaging the gross losses first and then subtracting the deductible gives the wrong result. Payment is a nonlinear function of each realized loss; that function must be applied before averaging.

For a discrete random variable \(X\) with mass function \(p_X\), its expectation is \(E[X]=\sum_x xp_X(x)\), provided the sum is well-defined. If \(\sum_x|x|p_X(x)\lt\infty\), the variable is integrable and its expectation is a finite real number. For an absolutely continuous law the corresponding definition is \(E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xf_X(x)\,dx\), under the analogous absolute-integrability condition.

For nonnegative variables, the sum or integral always has an unambiguous value in \([0,\infty]\). It need not be finite. For signed variables, define the positive and negative parts \(X^+=\max(X,0)\), \(X^-=\max(-X,0)\). Then \(X=X^+-X^-\), but \(E[X]\) is undefined if both part expectations are infinite. An infinity-minus-infinity cancellation cannot create a meaningful average.

In the illustrative loss model, \(E[X]=0(0.7)+100(0.2)+500(0.1)=70\). The expectation is neither the most likely loss, which is zero, nor necessarily a possible realized value. It is a probability-weighted financial quantity. Interpreting it as a repeated-sampling average requires a law of large numbers and its assumptions; the definition itself does not assert a convergence theorem.

4.2 Expectation of a function without finding a new law

Let \(Y=g(X)\), where \(g\) is a measurable real function. For a discrete law, the mass of \(Y\) at \(y\) is the sum of the masses of all \(x\) satisfying \(g(x)=y\). Regrouping the expectation of \(Y\) by these preimages gives

\[E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p_X(x).\]

Absolute convergence justifies regrouping for signed functions; nonnegativity suffices for nonnegative functions, even with infinite expectation. For a density-based law, the corresponding identity is \(E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx\). This is often called the law of the unconscious statistician. Its mathematical content is that integration against the distribution already accounts for how often each input occurs.

One construction proves the density identity without assuming that \(g\) is invertible. For an indicator function \(g(x)=\mathbf1_A(x)\), both sides equal \(P(X\in A)\). Finite nonnegative linear combinations of indicators inherit the identity by additivity. Approximate any nonnegative measurable function from below by such step functions and take increasing limits of their integrals. Finally apply the positive-minus-negative decomposition when the function is integrable. No transformation density is needed.

For constants \(a,b\) and integrable variables \(X,Z\), linearity gives \(E[aX+bZ]=aE[X]+bE[Z]\). In the finite setting, expand \(\sum_{x,z}(ax+bz)p(x,z)\) and collect each marginal sum. The same result for general integrable variables follows from linearity of the integral. Independence is unnecessary: the result is an algebraic property of averaging. Dependence becomes essential for products and variances, not for this sum.

An indicator \(\mathbf1_A\) equals one when event \(A\) occurs and zero otherwise. Its expectation is \(P(A)\). Thus a count \(N=\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf1_{A_j}\) satisfies \(E[N]=\sum_jP(A_j)\), with no independence assumption. Ten exposures each having claim probability 0.02 have expected claim-indicator count 0.2, even if their claim events are dependent. That expectation alone says nothing about the variability of the count.

4.3 Moments and dispersion

The \(r\)-th raw moment is \(E[X^r]\) when defined. If \(E[X^2]\lt\infty\), then the expectation \(\mu=E[X]\) is finite, and the variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]\). Finiteness of the first moment follows, for example, from \(|x|\le1+x^2\). Expanding the square and applying linearity gives

\[\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-2\mu E[X]+\mu^2=E[X^2]-\mu^2.\]

The variance is nonnegative because it averages a square. It is zero exactly when \(X=\mu\) with probability one: a nonnegative variable with zero expectation cannot be positive with positive probability. To see the last statement, if \(Z\gt0\) has positive probability, some event \(Z\ge1/n\) has positive probability, forcing \(E[Z]\ge P(Z\ge1/n)/n\gt0\).

For the loss model, \(E[X^2]=10000(0.2)+250000(0.1)=27000\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=27000-4900=22100\). The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{22100}\), approximately 148.66 in the original monetary unit. Variance carries squared monetary units. For \(aX+b\), subtracting its mean gives \(a(X-\mu)\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\). Changing units and changing location therefore affect dispersion differently.

Not every severity distribution has a finite mean or variance. The density \(f(x)=x^{-2}\) for \(x\ge1\), zero otherwise, integrates to one, but its mean is \(\int_1^\infty x^{-1}dx=\infty\). A finite median or finite observed sample average does not rescue that population mean. Moment existence is a model property that must be checked before using moment-based calculations.

4.4 Gross loss, insurer payment, and the averaging population

An ordinary deductible \(d\ge0\) leaves the first \(d\) units of loss with the insured. The insurer payment is \(Y=(X-d)^+=\max(X-d,0)\). Here \(X\) is gross loss and \(Y\) is payment per modeled loss exposure, including zero payments. For the three-point model with \(d=100\), the payments are zero, zero, and 400, with probabilities 0.7, 0.2, and 0.1. Thus \(E[Y]=40\).

The function evaluated at the mean gives \((E[X]-100)^+=0\), which differs from \(E[(X-100)^+]=40\). The positive tail drives payments even though the mean gross loss is below the deductible. More generally, for integrable \(X\), the inequality \((E[X]-d)^+\le E[(X-d)^+]\) follows directly: pointwise \((X-d)^+\ge X-d\) and \((X-d)^+\ge0\); average both inequalities and take the larger lower bound.

The per-payment distribution conditions on \(Y\gt0\); the per-loss distribution retains zero payments in the modeled population. If \(q=P(Y\gt0)\gt0\) and the mean payment is finite, then \(E[Y]=qE[Y\mid Y\gt0]\). In the discrete setting this is immediate by restricting the expectation sum to positive values and dividing their probabilities by \(q\). In this example \(q=0.1\), so the expected positive payment is 400, while the expected payment per loss is 40. Mixing these means creates a factor-of-ten error.

A franchise deductible instead pays the entire loss if it exceeds the threshold: \(Y_F=X\mathbf1_{\{X\gt d\}}\), under a strict-exceedance convention. In the example its mean is 50. The ordinary deductible pays 40 because it also withholds 100 from every payable loss. At a threshold atom, the choice between strict and weak exceedance must be stated explicitly.

4.5 Expectation as an area under a survival curve

For any nonnegative real number \(x\), its length can be written as \(x=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{\{x\gt t\}}dt\). Apply this identity to each realized loss. Interchanging nonnegative integrals gives the tail formula

\[E[X]=\int_0^\infty P(X\gt t)\,dt.\]

The interchange is valid without assuming finite expectation because all terms are nonnegative; this is the content of Tonelli’s theorem. In a finite discrete model it reduces to adding rectangle areas, making the mechanism visible without integration theory. For the three-point loss law, survival is 0.3 on \([0,100)\), 0.1 on \([100,500)\), and zero thereafter. Its area is \(100(0.3)+400(0.1)=70\).

Similarly \((X-d)^+=\int_d^\infty\mathbf1_{\{X\gt t\}}dt\), so \(E[(X-d)^+]=\int_d^\infty P(X\gt t)dt\). At \(d=100\), the retained area is \(400(0.1)=40\). Individual threshold points do not alter these integrals, but they do alter positive-payment probabilities when the loss law has atoms. The original program checks the finite distribution, its variance, and the two averaging populations using exact arithmetic.

"""Expectations before and after an ordinary deductible."""
from fractions import Fraction as F

law = {0: F(7, 10), 100: F(2, 10), 500: F(1, 10)}
assert sum(law.values()) == 1
mean = sum(x*p for x, p in law.items())
second = sum(x*x*p for x, p in law.items())
variance = second - mean*mean
payment = sum(max(x - 100, 0)*p for x, p in law.items())
positive = sum(p for x, p in law.items() if x > 100)
assert (mean, variance, payment) == (70, 22100, 40)
assert payment/positive == 400
print(f"E[X] = {mean}; Var(X) = {variance}")
print(f"E[(X - 100)+] per loss = {payment}")
print(f"Positive-payment probability = {positive}")
print(f"Mean per positive payment = {payment/positive}")

E[X] = 70; Var(X) = 22100
E[(X - 100)+] per loss = 40
Positive-payment probability = 1/10
Mean per positive payment = 400

You can now compute moments of a loss or payment directly, verify whether those moments exist, and identify the population behind each average.

4.6 Second moments and capped payments

Tail integration also determines the second moment of a nonnegative loss. For every realized \(x\ge0\), calculus gives \(x^2=\int_0^x2t\,dt\). Replacing the upper limit by an indicator and averaging nonnegative terms yields \(E[X^2]=2\int_0^\infty tP(X\gt t)dt\). In the three-point model this becomes \(2[0.3\int_0^{100}t\,dt+0.1\int_{100}^{500}t\,dt]=3000+24000=27000\). It agrees with the direct weighted-square calculation.

A payment cap \(u\ge0\) sets \(Y=\min(X,u)\). For a density-based gross loss, split the expectation at the cap: \(E[Y]=\int_0^u xf_X(x)dx+uP(X\ge u)\). The second term retains every loss beyond the cap, each paying the same amount. It must not be discarded merely because payments cannot exceed \(u\). A tail-area derivation gives the equivalent formula \(E[Y]=\int_0^uP(X\gt t)dt\), which also accommodates atoms.

With cap 200 in the three-point example, payments are 0, 100, and 200, so their mean is \(20+20=40\). Their second moment is \(2000+4000=6000\), and variance is \(6000-1600=4400\). The ordinary deductible of 100 also produced mean payment 40, but its payment variance is \(0.1(400^2)-40^2=14400\). Equal expected costs can conceal different variability because the two contracts reshape different parts of the loss distribution.

Finally, coinsurance multiplies a specified payment by an insurer share \(c\in[0,1]\). If the pre-coinsurance payment is \(Y\), the resulting mean is \(cE[Y]\) and variance is \(c^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)\). Whether coinsurance is applied before a cap or after it must be specified in the contract function; nonlinear operations generally do not commute. Subsequent chapters treat those combined payment rules explicitly.

4.7 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Express the chapter’s gross loss in hundreds of currency units. Find the new mean and variance.

Express the chapter’s gross loss in hundreds of currency units, \(Z=X/100\). Linearity gives \(E[Z]=0.7\); scaling the centered square gives \(\operatorname{Var}(Z)=22100/10000=2.21\). Standard deviation scales by 1/100, whereas variance scales by its square.

Exercise 2. For the three-point loss model, use ordinary deductible 50. Find per-loss and positive-payment means.

For \(d=50\), derive the mean payment. Payments at gross losses 0, 100, and 500 are 0, 50, and 450. Hence the per-loss mean is \(0.2(50)+0.1(450)=55\). Positive-payment probability is 0.3, so the positive-payment mean is \(55/0.3=550/3\).

Exercise 3. Five indicators equal one common Bernoulli variable of probability 0.1. Find the count mean and variance.

Suppose five claim indicators all equal the same Bernoulli variable \(I\), with \(P(I=1)=0.1\). The count is \(N=5I\), so \(E[N]=0.5\). This equals the sum of five marginal probabilities despite perfect dependence. Its variance is \(25(0.1)(0.9)=2.25\); independence would give a different variance.

Exercise 4. For density 2 x⁻³ on x>=1, verify normalization and find the first two moments.

For density \(f(x)=2x^{-3}\) on \(x\ge1\), verify normalization and moments. Its integral is \([-x^{-2}]_1^\infty=1\). The mean is \(2\int_1^\infty x^{-2}dx=2\). The second moment is \(2\int_1^\infty x^{-1}dx=\infty\), so no finite variance exists.

Exercise 5. Prove E[N]=sum over k>=0 of P(N>k) for a nonnegative integer count.

For a nonnegative integer-valued count \(N\), prove \(E[N]=\sum_{k=0}^\infty P(N\gt k)\). Each integer \(n\) equals \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\mathbf1_{\{n\gt k\}}\). Average this nonnegative series and interchange sums. Exactly \(n\) indicators equal one at outcome \(n\), which explains the identity.
