3.1 From an outcome to a numerical loss

A probability model may describe contracts, accidents, reporting decisions, and settlement amounts simultaneously. A calculation usually needs a numerical quantity extracted from that outcome. What is the probability that a one-year gross loss exceeds a threshold? The event is numerical, but the probability still comes from the underlying experiment.

A random variable \(X\) is a measurable function from the sample space \(\Omega\) into the real numbers. Measurability means that \(\{\omega:X(\omega)\le x\}\) is an event for every real threshold \(x\). In a finite space with all subsets allowed, every numerical function is measurable. The outcome \(\omega\), the function \(X\), and its realized value \(x\) are different objects. Randomness resides in which outcome occurs.

For a fair die, the face value is one random variable, and the indicator of an even face is another. The latter equals one on \(\{2,4,6\}\) and zero otherwise. Different outcomes may yield the same numerical value; probabilities for all such outcomes must be added. This aggregation creates the distribution, or probability law, of the random variable.

For insurance notation, \(X\ge0\) will ordinarily represent gross loss measured in a stated monetary unit. It may be a loss per exposure, including zeros, or a positive loss given that a claim occurred. These are different distributions. A model that says “loss is exponential” must specify whether it refers to all exposure outcomes or only positive severities.

3.2 Discrete laws and probability mass

A random variable is discrete if \(P(X\in D)=1\) for some finite or countable set \(D\). Its probability mass function, abbreviated PMF, is \(p_X(x)=P(X=x)\). The masses are nonnegative and sum to one over \(D\). For every Borel set \(A\subseteq\mathbb R\), the event \(\{X\in A\}\) differs from the disjoint union of \(\{X=x\}\), \(x\in D\cap A\), only by a measurable event of probability zero. Countable additivity therefore gives

\[P(X\in A)=\sum_{x\in D\cap A}p_X(x).\]

A Borel set belongs to the sigma-algebra generated by open intervals. All intervals and finite or countable unions used in this chapter are Borel sets.

Consider an original illustrative gross-loss model: \(P(X=0)=0.7\), \(P(X=100)=0.2\), and \(P(X=500)=0.1\). The probability of a positive loss is \(0.3\); the probability of a loss exceeding 100 is \(0.1\). Conditional on a positive loss, the two positive masses become \(2/3\) and \(1/3\). Dividing by \(0.3\) changes the reference population from all exposures to positive-loss exposures.

The support of this discrete model can be described as the values with positive mass, namely \(\{0,100,500\}\). When presenting a PMF formula, specify where it is nonzero and set it to zero elsewhere. A formula such as \(p_X(k)=c(k+1)\) does not define a law until the allowed integers are supplied. On \(k=0,1,2\), normalization gives \(c(1+2+3)=1\), hence \(c=1/6\). On all nonnegative integers, no positive constant could normalize that expression.

Countably infinite support requires a convergent sum. For \(p_X(k)=2^{-(k+1)}\), \(k=0,1,\ldots\), the geometric series gives total mass \((1/2)/(1-1/2)=1\). The tail \(P(X\ge m)=\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}2^{-(k+1)}=2^{-m}\). A variable can therefore have infinitely many possible values while remaining discrete. “Discrete” does not mean that its values are equally likely or that its support must be finite.

3.3 Density describes probability per unit length

A law is absolutely continuous if a nonnegative integrable function \(f_X\) satisfies \(P(X\in A)=\int_A f_X(x)\,dx\) for every measurable set \(A\). Such a function is a probability density function, or PDF. Normalization requires \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1\). The density has reciprocal units: if loss is measured in dollars, density is measured per dollar.

A point has zero length, so \(P(X=x)=0\) for every particular value under an absolutely continuous law. The value \(f_X(x)\) is not the probability of that point. For the uniform law on \((0,0.1)\), the density is ten throughout the interval, yet its integral is one. Densities can exceed one because probability is area, not height. At points of continuity, \(P(x\lt X\le x+h)=f_X(x)h+o(h)\) as positive \(h\) approaches zero; this follows by averaging the continuous integrand over a shrinking interval.

Take \(f_X(x)=2x\) for \(0\lt x\lt1\), zero elsewhere. Its total integral is \([x^2]_0^1=1\). For \(0\le a\le b\le1\), \(P(a\lt X\le b)=b^2-a^2\). In particular, \(P(X\gt1/2)=3/4\). Larger values carry more density, so the upper half contains more probability than the lower half. Whether either endpoint is included makes no difference here because both endpoints have zero mass.

Changing a density at one point, or on any set of zero length, leaves every probability unchanged. Thus density formulas are versions of the same law, rather than uniquely meaningful point values everywhere. This fact becomes important when conditioning on continuous variables. Some distributions are neither discrete nor absolutely continuous; a mixed distribution below already requires both point masses and a density component.

3.4 One distribution function for every real random variable

The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, is \(F_X(x)=P(X\le x)\). It exists for discrete, absolutely continuous, and mixed laws. For \(a\lt b\), the event \(\{X\le b\}\) splits into \(\{X\le a\}\) and \(\{a\lt X\le b\}\), giving

\[P(a\lt X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).\]

Every CDF is nondecreasing, lies between zero and one, tends to zero as \(x\to-\infty\), and tends to one as \(x\to\infty\). Monotonicity follows from event inclusion. The limits follow from continuity of probability for monotone event sequences. For increasing events, disjoint successive differences and countable additivity prove this continuity; decreasing-event continuity follows by taking complements. Applying the decreasing version to \(\{X\le x+1/n\}\) proves right continuity, \(F_X(x+1/n)\to F_X(x)\).

The left limit \(F_X(x-)=\lim_{t\uparrow x}F_X(t)=P(X\lt x)\) follows from increasing-event continuity. Therefore the jump size is

\[P(X=x)=F_X(x)-F_X(x-).\]

This identity keeps strict and weak inequalities straight. The survival probability \(P(X\gt x)\) equals \(1-F_X(x)\); \(P(X\ge x)\) equals \(1-F_X(x-)\). In the three-point loss example, \(F_X(100)=0.9\) but \(F_X(100-)=0.7\). Consequently \(P(X\gt100)=0.1\) while \(P(X\ge100)=0.3\).

For an absolutely continuous law, \(F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t)\,dt\), so the fundamental theorem of calculus gives \(F_X'(x)=f_X(x)\) wherever the density is continuous, and almost everywhere more generally. The converse needs absolute continuity. A continuous CDF alone need not have a PDF; continuity merely excludes jumps and hence point masses. We will work with discrete, density-based, and explicitly mixed models.

3.5 A zero mass with a continuous positive loss

Suppose a hypothetical exposure has zero loss with probability \(0.7\). With the remaining probability \(0.3\), its positive loss has density \(0.5e^{-0.5x}\), \(x\gt0\). This conditional density integrates to one. Combining the zero mass and positive component gives

\[F_X(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\lt0,\\0.7+0.3(1-e^{-0.5x}),&x\ge0.\end{cases}\]

The right-hand expression equals \(0.7\) at zero, while the left limit is zero. Its jump is therefore the prescribed zero-loss probability. On the positive half-line the unconditional density component is \(0.15e^{-0.5x}\), whose integral is \(0.3\). Calling this component a complete PDF would lose seventy percent of the law. The positive conditional density instead divides it by the positive-loss probability.

The original program evaluates this CDF and checks its jump, monotonicity on a finite grid, and selected interval probabilities. It computes \(P(X\gt2)=0.3e^{-1}\) and \(P(0\lt X\le2)=0.3(1-e^{-1})\). Those probabilities sum to \(0.3\), leaving the zero mass separate. Numerical grid checks illustrate the derived law; the formulas prove its properties for all thresholds.

"""CDF jumps and a mixed loss distribution."""
from math import exp, isclose

atom = 0.7
rate = 0.5


def cdf(x):
    return 0.0 if x < 0 else atom + (1 - atom)*(1 - exp(-rate*x))


assert cdf(-1) == 0
assert cdf(0) == atom
assert isclose(1 - cdf(2), 0.3*exp(-1))
assert all(cdf(i/10) <= cdf((i + 1)/10) for i in range(-10, 100))
print(f"P(X = 0) = {cdf(0):.6f}")
print(f"P(X > 2) = {1 - cdf(2):.6f}")
print(f"P(0 < X <= 2) = {cdf(2) - cdf(0):.6f}")

P(X = 0) = 0.700000
P(X > 2) = 0.110364
P(0 < X <= 2) = 0.189636

You can now specify a numerical loss law without confusing point probability, density, and cumulative probability. Expectations will use these same representations to weight possible financial outcomes.

3.6 A probability threshold can fall inside a jump

A quantile answers the inverse question: which loss threshold accumulates a specified probability? For \(0\lt p\lt1\), define the generalized quantile \(Q(p)=\inf\{x:F_X(x)\ge p\}\). The infimum is the greatest lower bound of the indicated set. The CDF limits ensure a finite crossing threshold for every such \(p\). A jump may cross several probability levels at the same loss, so a usual algebraic inverse need not exist.

In the mixed model, \(Q(p)=0\) for \(0\lt p\le0.7\). For \(0.7\lt p\lt1\), solve \(p=1-0.3e^{-0.5x}\), obtaining \(Q(p)=-2\log[(1-p)/0.3]\). Thus the median, the quantile at probability one-half, is zero, although positive losses can be arbitrarily large. The 0.9 quantile is \(2\log3\). The zero atom creates the flat part of the quantile function, and the continuous tail supplies its increasing part.

The defining inequalities are \(F_X(Q(p)-)\le p\le F_X(Q(p))\). At a jump, neither inequality has to be equality. For the median just found, the left and right CDF values are zero and 0.7. Describing the median as a point with exactly fifty percent probability below it would fail in this example. A quantile selects a threshold at which the requested cumulative level is reached or exceeded.

These inequalities follow from the crossing construction. Every threshold below \(Q(p)\) has CDF less than \(p\); taking their increasing limit gives the left inequality. Right continuity ensures the crossing level is attained at \(Q(p)\), giving the right inequality. This is another reason the CDF’s endpoint convention is part of its definition.

3.7 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. A CDF is 0 below 0, 0.6 on [0,2), 0.9 on [2,5), and 1 thereafter. Recover its masses and compare P(0<X<=2) with P(0<=X<2).

A CDF equals zero below zero, 0.6 on \([0,2)\), 0.9 on \([2,5)\), and one on \([5,\infty)\). Its jumps give masses \(0.6\) at zero, \(0.3\) at two, and \(0.1\) at five. Thus \(P(0\lt X\le2)=0.3\), whereas \(P(0\le X\lt2)=0.6\). Endpoint conventions change the event.

Exercise 2. Normalize f(x)=c x² on (0,2), zero elsewhere, and find P(X>1).

Find \(c\) making \(f(x)=cx^2\), \(0\lt x\lt2\), a PDF, and find \(P(X\gt1)\). Integration gives \(1=c[x^3/3]_0^2=8c/3\), so \(c=3/8\). The required probability is \((3/8)[x^3/3]_1^2=7/8\).

Exercise 3. For X uniform on (0,0.1), calculate P(0.02<X<0.05) and explain why density ten is permissible.

For a uniform loss on \((0,0.1)\), calculate \(P(0.02\lt X\lt0.05)\). The interval has length 0.03 and density ten, so the probability is 0.3. A density height of ten does not violate the probability axioms; the integral over the whole support is one.

Exercise 4. In the mixed model of Section 3.5, derive P(X>2 given X>0).

In the chapter’s mixed model, derive \(P(X\gt2\mid X\gt0)\). Since \(\{X\gt2\}\subseteq\{X\gt0\}\), the conditional probability is \(0.3e^{-1}/0.3=e^{-1}\). The zero mass disappears only because conditioning explicitly removes zero-loss outcomes.

Exercise 5. Can a CDF equal 0.4 at two and 0.3 at three? Prove your answer.

Can a CDF equal 0.4 at two and 0.3 at three? No. The event \(\{X\le2\}\) is contained in \(\{X\le3\}\), so its probability cannot be greater. Equivalently \(F(3)-F(2)\) would give the negative interval probability \(-0.1\).
