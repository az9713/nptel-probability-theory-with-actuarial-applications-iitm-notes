Mixtures and compound models

13.1 Selection and addition are different mechanisms

A portfolio can be heterogeneous because each policyholder belongs to a different risk class, or because each policyholder produces a random number of payments. These mechanisms generate different distributions. A mixture selects a conditional law through a random parameter or class. A compound model adds a random number of severity variables. Conditioning handles both, but the variable being conditioned on has a different role.

Consider an original comparison. A policyholder is selected from low-risk and high-risk classes with probabilities \(0.8\) and \(0.2\). If annual claim count is Poisson with means one and four in those classes, the unconditional count is a mixture of Poisson laws. Alternatively, a count \(N\) can be Poisson with mean two while each payment is one or three units. Their sum is a compound Poisson aggregate. Selecting an annual frequency parameter does not add a random number of frequency parameters.

A further distinction is terminological. A mixed discrete-continuous distribution has both point masses and a density component. A mixture of distributions need not have that form: mixing two continuous normal laws remains continuous. Conversely, a deductible can create a point mass without any latent risk class. Keeping the construction explicit resolves this ambiguity.

13.2 Finite mixtures by partitioning events

Let \(Z\) be a class label taking values \(1,\ldots,m\), with probabilities \(w_j\geq0\) summing to one. Let \(F_j(x)=P(X\leq x\mid Z=j)\) for classes with positive weight. Partitioning the event \(X\leq x\) across the disjoint classes gives

\[F_X(x)=\sum_{j=1}^m w_jF_j(x).\]

If every component has density \(f_j\), differentiation gives \(f_X(x)=\sum_jw_jf_j(x)\). If components have probability mass functions, the same weighted sum applies to their masses. Class membership must be chosen once for the observation represented by \(X\); resampling a class before each payment defines a different hierarchical model.

For conditional means \(\mu_j\) and variances \(\sigma_j^2\), total expectation gives \(\mu=\sum_jw_j\mu_j\). Total variance gives \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_jw_j\sigma_j^2+\sum_jw_j(\mu_j-\mu)^2\). The first term averages component variances; the second records differences between component means. Thus a weighted average of variances alone is generally incorrect.

Take exponential severities with rates one and \(1/4\), weighted \(0.8\) and \(0.2\). Their means are one and four, and variances one and sixteen. The mixture mean is \(1.6\); average within-class variance is four. Between-class variance is \(0.8(1-1.6)^2+0.2(4-1.6)^2=1.44\), so total variance is \(5.44\). A single exponential distribution with mean \(1.6\) would have variance \(2.56\) and a different tail.

The mixture survival function is \(0.8e^{-x}+0.2e^{-x/4}\). At large \(x\), the high-mean component dominates despite its smaller weight. The ratio of the first contribution to the second is \(4e^{-3x/4}\), tending to zero. A small subgroup can therefore dominate a portfolio tail while contributing modestly to its central mass.

13.3 Point masses and continuous components

Let a nonnegative payment satisfy \(P(X=0)=q\), where \(0\leq q<1\), and conditional on positivity let it have density \(g\) on \((0,\infty)\). Its CDF is zero below zero and \(q+(1-q)\int_0^xg(y)dy\) for \(x\geq0\). The unconditional continuous density is \((1-q)g(x)\), whose integral is \(1-q\). Normalizing it to one would erase the zero probability. The boundary case \(q=1\) is the deterministic zero payment; it has no positive-payment conditional distribution.

If the positive payment has mean \(\mu_+\) and second moment \(m_{2,+}\), then \(E[X]=(1-q)\mu_+\) and \(E[X^2]=(1-q)m_{2,+}\). Subtracting the squared mean gives \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=(1-q)m_{2,+}-(1-q)^2\mu_+^2\). Equivalently, total variance gives \((1-q)\sigma_+^2+q(1-q)\mu_+^2\). Both representations include variation caused by whether a payment occurs.

For \(q=0.7\) and exponential positive payments of mean two, \(E[X]=0.6\), \(E[X^2]=0.3(8)=2.4\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=2.04\). The zero-inflated variable is neither exponential nor purely discrete. Its point mass can represent no claim, a deductible eliminating small losses, or another explicitly modeled mechanism; the same observed law does not identify which mechanism generated it.

13.4 Mixing Poisson rates creates overdispersion

Let \(\Lambda\geq0\) be a random rate, and conditional on \(\Lambda=\lambda\), let \(N\) be Poisson with mean \(\lambda\). The unconditional mass is \(P(N=n)=E[e^{-\Lambda}\Lambda^n/n!]\) for nonnegative integer \(n\). If \(E[\Lambda^2]<\infty\), the conditional Poisson mean and variance both equal \(\Lambda\), so

\[E[N]=E[\Lambda],\qquad \operatorname{Var}(N)=E[\Lambda]+\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda).\]

A nonconstant rate produces variance strictly larger than the mean, called overdispersion relative to the Poisson model. For the two-class rates one and four, \(E[N]=1.6\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\Lambda)=1.44\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=3.04\). This excess variance arises from heterogeneity, not from a change to the Poisson variance within either class.

A continuous example can be integrated exactly. Let \(\Lambda\) have gamma density \(b^a\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}/\Gamma(a)\), with shape \(a>0\), rate \(b>0\), and \(\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-t}dt\). Multiplying by the conditional Poisson mass and substituting \(t=(b+1)\lambda\) gives

\[P(N=n)=\frac{\Gamma(n+a)}{\Gamma(a)n!}\left(\frac{b}{b+1}\right)^a\left(\frac1{b+1}\right)^n.\]

This is a negative-binomial parameterization with shape \(a\), including noninteger shapes. The normalization is guaranteed by integrating a conditional probability distribution against a probability density. Gamma moments give \(E[N]=a/b\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=a/b+a/b^2\). Different negative-binomial conventions use different meanings for the count or success parameter, so the explicit mass is more informative than the family name alone.

13.5 Random sums add severities

Randomizing a binomial success probability supplies a second mixture mechanism. Let \(P\) be uniform on \((0,1)\), and conditional on \(P=p\), let \(B\) be binomial with fixed trial count \(n\geq1\). Then \(E[B\mid P]=nP\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(B\mid P)=nP(1-P)\). Since \(E[P]=1/2\), \(E[P^2]=1/3\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(P)=1/12\), total moments give \(E[B]=n/2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(B)=n/6+n^2/12=n(n+2)/12\). The quadratic term comes from using one shared success probability for all trials.

The full law is especially informative. Integrating the conditional binomial mass gives \(P(B=k)=\binom nk\int_0^1p^k(1-p)^{n-k}dp=1/(n+1)\), using the factorial integral derived for order statistics. Thus \(B\) is uniform on the integers zero through \(n\), rather than binomial with success probability one half. If a fresh independent uniform probability were drawn separately for each trial, the unconditional trials would instead be independent Bernoulli variables with success probability one half. Drawing the parameter once or repeatedly changes the joint model.

Let \(N\) be a nonnegative integer count, and let \(Y_1,Y_2,\ldots\) be iid severities independent of \(N\). Define \(S=\sum_{i=1}^NY_i\), with the empty sum equal to zero. Conditional on \(N=n\), the aggregate is a fixed sum of \(n\) independent severities. If the common severity law is \(F_Y\), write \(F_Y^{*n}\) for its n-fold convolution and let \(F_Y^{*0}\) be the point mass at zero. Partitioning on the count gives \(F_S(s)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty P(N=n)F_Y^{*n}(s)\).

A compound law is therefore mathematically a mixture over convolution laws, but the generative operation is addition. This observation reconciles the terminology without conflating the models. A severity mixture selects one severity law; a compound model selects how many severity variables to add.

Suppose \(E[N^2]<\infty\), \(E[Y_1^2]<\infty\), and set \(\mu=E[Y_1]\), \(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(Y_1)\). Independence gives \(E[S\mid N]=N\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S\mid N)=N\sigma^2\). The total-moment identities yield \(E[S]=E[N]\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S)=E[N]\sigma^2+\operatorname{Var}(N)\mu^2\). If count and severity depend on a shared risk class, these conditional expressions require further conditioning and cannot be used automatically.

For Poisson count of mean two and severity equally likely one or three, \(\mu=2\), \(\sigma^2=1\), so aggregate mean is four and variance is ten. With strictly positive severities, \(P(S=0)=P(N=0)=e^{-2}\). If severities themselves can equal zero, zero aggregate can also arise from a positive count, so that equality no longer holds. The count distribution and payment definition must match.

A shared risk parameter can combine mixture and compounding in a single hierarchy. Suppose \(Z\) is selected once for a policy, then conditional on \(Z=z\), the count has mean \(\nu(z)\) and variance \(v(z)\). Given that class, severities are iid with mean \(\mu(z)\) and variance \(\sigma^2(z)\), and the count is independent of the severity sequence within class. Conditioning first on both \(Z\) and \(N\), then on \(Z\), gives \(E[S\mid Z]=\nu(Z)\mu(Z)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S\mid Z)=\nu(Z)\sigma^2(Z)+v(Z)\mu(Z)^2\). A final application of total variance adds \(\operatorname{Var}(\nu(Z)\mu(Z))\) to the average conditional variance.

The extra between-class term is essential. In two equally likely classes, suppose conditional count is Poisson with rates one and three, while every severity is deterministically one in the first class and two in the second. Conditional aggregate means are one and six, and conditional variances are one and twelve. The unconditional mean is \(3.5\); average within-class variance is \(6.5\), and between-class variance is \(6.25\). Total variance is \(12.75\). Combining an unconditional count mean with an independently selected unconditional severity law would define another model, because the actual policy class simultaneously affects count and payment size.

The hierarchy clarifies what must be specified before calculation. A risk class drawn once per portfolio creates shared dependence across policies; a class drawn separately for each policy produces another joint law; a class redrawn separately for every claim produces yet another severity mechanism. Identical marginal class weights do not make these constructions interchangeable. Their effect appears in conditional means, conditional variances, and the dependence terms between different payments.

The construction now determines the calculation: condition on a selected parameter for heterogeneity, or condition on a count for addition. The same laws of total moments support both, while the conditional model identifies which means and variances belong in them.

13.6 Exercises with worked solutions

Identify the random selection or addition mechanism before calculating. A mixture averages conditional laws, while a random sum first forms a fixed-count convolution and then averages over the count.

Exercise 1. A mixture selects deterministic losses 1 and 5 with equal probability. Find its variance and explain why average component variance fails.

Each component variance is zero. The mixture mean is three, and its variance is \((1/2)(1-3)^2+(1/2)(5-3)^2=4\). All randomness lies between the component means, so omitting that term would predict zero incorrectly.

Exercise 2. Let X have zero mass 0.4 and otherwise be uniform on (0,2). Find its CDF, mean, and variance.

The CDF is zero below zero, \(0.4+0.3x\) for \(0\leq x<2\), and one thereafter. Positive-component moments are one and \(4/3\). Thus \(E[X]=0.6\), \(E[X^2]=0.8\), and variance is \(0.8-0.36=0.44\).

Exercise 3. A mixed Poisson model has E[Λ]=3 and Var(Λ)=2. Find its mean and variance, and state whether an ordinary Poisson model can match both.

The count mean is three and variance five. An ordinary Poisson distribution has equal mean and variance, so none can match both numbers. This does not establish the mixed Poisson model uniquely; other count constructions can share those moments.

Exercise 4. Let N be equally likely 0 or 2, and independent severities be equally likely 1 or 2. Enumerate the aggregate law and check its variance.

The masses are \(P(S=0)=1/2\), \(P(S=2)=1/8\), \(P(S=3)=1/4\), \(P(S=4)=1/8\). The mean is \(1.5\), and the second moment is \(4/8+9/4+16/8=4.75\). Hence variance is \(4.75-2.25=2.5\). Independently, the random-sum formula gives \(E[N](1/4)+\operatorname{Var}(N)(1.5)^2=0.25+2.25=2.5\).

Exercise 5. Independent Poisson count has mean λ, and each severity is Bernoulli(p). Find the zero-aggregate probability without assuming every count creates a payment.

Given \(N=n\), all severities are zero with probability \((1-p)^n\). Averaging gives \(e^{-\lambda}\sum_{n\geq0}[\lambda(1-p)]^n/n!=e^{-\lambda p}\). This exceeds \(e^{-\lambda}\) when \(p<1\), because positive counts can generate no positive payment.

13.7 Enumerating a compound distribution

The program constructs the finite aggregate in Exercise 4 using exact convolution. It verifies both moments and contrasts the mixed Poisson mean and variance. The finite enumeration supplies a reproducible check rather than a simulation estimate.

"""Exact finite compound model and a mixed Poisson moment comparison."""
from collections import defaultdict
from fractions import Fraction as F


def convolve(left, right):
    result = defaultdict(F)
    for x, px in left.items():
        for y, py in right.items():
            result[x+y] += px*py
    return dict(result)


severity = {1: F(1, 2), 2: F(1, 2)}
two_claims = convolve(severity, severity)
aggregate = {0: F(1, 2)}
aggregate.update({s: p/2 for s, p in two_claims.items()})
mean = sum(s*p for s, p in aggregate.items())
second = sum(s*s*p for s, p in aggregate.items())
variance = second-mean*mean
assert sum(aggregate.values()) == 1
assert mean == F(3, 2)
assert variance == F(5, 2)
rate_mean = F(4, 5)*1 + F(1, 5)*4
rate_var = F(4, 5)*(1-rate_mean)**2 + F(1, 5)*(4-rate_mean)**2
assert rate_mean + rate_var == F(76, 25)
print("aggregate pmf = " + ", ".join(
    f"{s}:{aggregate[s]}" for s in sorted(aggregate)))
print(f"aggregate mean = {mean}; variance = {variance}")
print(f"mixed Poisson mean = {float(rate_mean):.2f}")
print(f"mixed Poisson variance = {float(rate_mean+rate_var):.2f}")

aggregate pmf = 0:1/2, 2:1/8, 3:1/4, 4:1/8
aggregate mean = 3/2; variance = 5/2
mixed Poisson mean = 1.60
mixed Poisson variance = 3.04
