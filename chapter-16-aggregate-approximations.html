Aggregate approximations and model selection

16.1 Choose a model before choosing an approximation

An aggregate probability calculation can fail before any arithmetic begins. A sum over a fixed number of contracts differs from a sum over a random number of claims. A Poisson count differs from a Poisson aggregate payment. A moment formula can be exact even when a normal tail calculation is poor. Model selection starts by identifying what is counted and what each summand measures.

For a fixed-count model, let \(T_n=\sum_{i=1}^nY_i\), where \(n\) is a fixed integer and the \(Y_i\) are annual payments from specified contracts or severities from specified events. For a collective model, let \(S=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_i\), where \(N\) is a random nonnegative integer. The same summation symbol hides a different probability model unless the index is stated.

In the fixed independent case, \(E[T_n]=\sum_iE[Y_i]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T_n)=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(Y_i)\). Identical distributions simplify these to \(nm\) and \(nv\), where \(m\) and \(v\) are the common mean and variance. A normal approximation then uses these moments, subject to an applicable central limit theorem.

For a random count independent of independent, identically distributed severities, conditioning gives \(E[S\mid N]=Nm\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S\mid N)=Nv\). Hence \(E[S]=E[N]m\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S)=E[N]v+\operatorname{Var}(N)m^2\). Chapter 19 develops these formulas and their assumptions fully. They are derived here to avoid treating a random count as its expected value.

16.2 Preserve an exact family when one is available

A count of successes among \(n\) independent trials with common probability \(p\) is binomial. Its exact tail is a finite sum of \(\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}\). If each success produces a fixed payment \(b>0\), total payment is \(bB\), where \(B\) is that binomial count. It is a scaled count, not a continuous normal loss.

A Poisson event count \(N\) with mean \(\lambda\) gives aggregate \(bN\) when all severities are exactly \(b\). If severities are random, the aggregate is generally compound Poisson rather than Poisson. For example, if a claim can pay 1 or 2, one event can add either amount. A Poisson distribution on monetary units would incorrectly impose a specific relationship between its mean and variance.

Independent exponential severities with common rate \(\beta>0\) have an exact gamma sum for fixed \(n\): \(T_n\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(n,\beta)\), using shape and rate. The transform of one severity is \(\beta/(\beta-t)\) for \(t<\beta\); independence raises it to power \(n\), giving the gamma transform. If \(n\) is an integer, integration by parts gives the exact survival

\[P(T_n>x)=e^{-\beta x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(\beta x)^k}{k!},\qquad x\geq0.\]

The recurrence arises by integrating the gamma density once by parts, leaving a lower-shape gamma tail; repeated reduction ends at the exponential survival. A tractable exact tail is usually a better benchmark than a normal approximation. Numerical stability still matters: very large powers and factorials should be evaluated through suitable recurrences or logarithms.

16.3 Rare-event approximation changes a count law

Suppose \(B_n\) is binomial with \(p_n=\lambda/n\), for fixed \(\lambda>0\). For any fixed nonnegative integer \(k\),

\[P(B_n=k)=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}(1-\lambda/n)^n(1-\lambda/n)^{-k}.\]

As \(n\to\infty\), the first and last factors tend to one and the middle exponential factor tends to \(e^{-\lambda}\). The limit is the Poisson mass \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\). This is a rare-event asymptotic regime: many trials, small probability, fixed expected count. It is different from the normal regime where the count's variance becomes large.

Original worked example. For 2000 independent contracts with event probability 0.001 each, the expected event count is 2. A Poisson approximation with mean 2 retains the marked right skew and zero-count probability. A normal approximation with mean 2 and variance about 2 assigns appreciable probability to negative counts and has no zero atom. “Two thousand contracts” does not imply two thousand effective claims.

If \(p\) is close to one, approximate failures \(n-B\) instead of successes when appropriate. The count law's support and its location matter. Rules such as checking \(np\) and \(n(1-p)\) are practical diagnostics, not universal guarantees for every tail probability. Exact computation or a quantitative error bound is needed when the requested tolerance is consequential.

16.4 A compound normal approximation has its own limit

For a compound Poisson aggregate with intensity \(\lambda\), nonnegative severity \(Y\), and finite positive raw second moment \(r=E[Y^2]\), the exact moments are \(\lambda m\) and \(\lambda r\), where \(m=E[Y]\). As \(\lambda\to\infty\) with the severity law fixed,

\[\frac{S_\lambda-\lambda m}{\sqrt{\lambda r}}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathcal N(0,1).\]

To see the scaling, the characteristic function of \(S_\lambda\) is \(\exp\{\lambda(\varphi_Y(t)-1)\}\), derived by conditioning on the Poisson count. Finite second moment gives \(\varphi_Y(s)=1+ims-rs^2/2+o(s^2)\). Substitute \(s=t/\sqrt{\lambda r}\) and center. The linear terms cancel, the quadratic term becomes \(-t^2/2\), and the remainder tends to zero. The characteristic-function continuity theorem completes the argument.

This is a limit over increasing intensity, not a theorem that every random sum becomes normal when its expected count is large. As a counterexample, let \(N_n\) equal zero or \(2n\) with equal probabilities and take deterministic severity 1. After centering and standardizing, the aggregate takes values −1 and 1 for every \(n\). Its expected count tends to infinity, but its standardized law never approaches a normal distribution.

For a fixed compound Poisson intensity, a normal approximation uses \(\sigma_S=\sqrt{\lambda r}\), not \(\sqrt{\lambda v}\). A continuity correction is appropriate only if aggregate payment is on a known monetary lattice. Arbitrary continuous or mixed severities do not acquire a universal half-unit correction merely because the count is discrete.

16.5 Check support, atoms, and the requested tail

Original worked comparison. Let severities be exponential with mean 100. Then \(m=100\), \(r=20000\), and a Poisson count of mean 2 gives aggregate mean 200 and variance 40000. A fitted normal law has standard deviation 200 and assigns \(\Phi(-1)\approx0.1587\) probability below zero. The true aggregate is nonnegative and has zero atom \(e^{-2}\approx0.1353\). Those discrepancies warn that the distributional shape is poorly represented near zero.

With intensity 200, the same severity law gives mean 20000 and standard deviation 2000. The normal lower-tail probability below zero is then \(\Phi(-10)\), while the zero atom is \(e^{-200}\). Both are negligible on ordinary probability scales. This removes one obvious mismatch, but it does not establish accuracy in the far upper tail. Moment agreement and support diagnostics are necessary checks, not a proof of approximation quality.

Where third moments exist, compound Poisson skewness is \(E[Y^3]/(\sqrt\lambda\,r^{3/2})\). This follows by differentiating the logarithm of the characteristic function near zero, which is permitted by the finite third moment. A nonvanishing characteristic function has a continuous logarithm in a neighborhood of zero. The third cumulant is \(\lambda E[Y^3]\), and standardization divides by \((\lambda r)^{3/2}\). Exponential severity has raw third moment \(6\theta^3\), so skewness is \(3/\sqrt{2\lambda}\). It is about 1.5 at intensity 2 and 0.15 at intensity 200. This diagnostic measures asymmetry; it is not a certified tail error bound.

A simulation can benchmark a complicated contract model, provided its sampling mechanism and Monte Carlo uncertainty are stated. For an event indicator estimated from \(M\) independent simulated years, variance of the estimated probability is \(p(1-p)/M\). If the event is rare, observing zero occurrences does not prove the probability is zero. Exact finite sums and deterministic quadrature avoid that sampling uncertainty when available.

16.6 An operational sequence for aggregate calculations

First identify whether summands are event severities, positive payments, or annual contract totals. State whether the number of summands is fixed or random. Define the payment transformation before computing its moments. Check the independence assumptions separately for severities and count.

Next seek an exact family or a tractable finite computation. A fixed normal sum, a scaled binomial count, a fixed sum of common-rate gamma severities, and some discrete convolutions admit direct answers. If an approximation is needed, identify its limiting regime: rare-event Poisson, fixed-count central limit, or increasing-intensity compound normal.

Finally translate the requested event into the modeled variable's units. Apply a lattice correction only at actual discrete boundaries. Report the exact moment calculation separately from the approximate probability, and compare a benchmark at relevant thresholds. This procedure guards against errors that no increase in numerical precision can repair.

16.7 The individual model contains occurrence uncertainty

A fixed contract count does not mean a fixed claim count. Let \(I_i\) indicate whether contract \(i\) generates one payment, with \(P(I_i=1)=p\). Let \(B_i\) be the corresponding positive payment severity, independent of \(I_i\), with mean \(b\) and variance \(s^2\). Annual contract payment is \(Y_i=I_iB_i\). It contains a zero atom even if positive severity is continuous.

Independence gives \(E[Y_i]=pb\) and \(E[Y_i^2]=p(s^2+b^2)\). Therefore \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i)=ps^2+p(1-p)b^2\). For \(n\) independent identical contracts, aggregate variance is \(nps^2+np(1-p)b^2\). The second term already incorporates whether each contract pays. Adding a random-count variance term again would double count occurrence risk.

The same model can be represented with a binomial number of positive payments and independent positive severities. Its random-sum variance is \(np s^2+np(1-p)b^2\), exactly agreeing with the individual representation. Replacing the binomial count by Poisson with mean \(np\) changes this to \(np(s^2+b^2)\), an increase of \(np^2b^2\). The approximation discrepancy in variance has an explicit source.

With \(n=1000,p=0.01,b=100,s^2=10000\), the exact individual aggregate mean is 1000 and variance is 199000. The Poisson approximation preserves the mean and gives variance 200000. This small moment discrepancy does not establish that every tail probability is accurate, but it makes the approximation's effect inspectable. When \(p\) is substantial, the omitted binomial factor can matter much more.

16.8 Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 1. Fixed count or random count? Payments have mean 5 and variance 9. Compare exactly 100 payments with a Poisson number of mean 100.

The fixed sum has mean 500 and variance 900. The Poisson aggregate has the same mean and variance \(100(9+25)=3400\). Their standard deviations are 30 and \(\sqrt{3400}\). Treating the random count as fixed discards the variance 2500 contributed by frequency fluctuation.

Exercise 2. Exact gamma benchmark. Two independent exponential losses have rate 1. Compute P(total>3) exactly and write its normal approximation.

The gamma survival is \(e^{-3}(1+3)=4e^{-3}\approx0.19915\). The exact mean is 2 and variance is 2. A normal approximation gives \(1-\Phi((3-2)/\sqrt2)\approx0.23975\). The discrepancy is appreciable at this small shape. No continuity correction applies to continuous losses.

Exercise 3. Rare successes. For B binomial with n=1000,p=0.002, approximate P(B=0) by a Poisson law and identify the exact expression.

The Poisson mean is 2, so its zero probability is \(e^{-2}\). The exact probability is \((0.998)^{1000}\). Their proximity follows from \(1000\log(1-0.002)\approx-2\); the next log-series term is −0.002. This comparison concerns this event, not every relative tail error.

Exercise 4. Correct monetary lattice. Each of a Poisson number of events pays exactly 250. The count mean is 40. Approximate P(S≥12500).

The event is \(N\geq50\), whose corrected count boundary is 49.5. Thus the approximation is \(1-\Phi((49.5-40)/\sqrt{40})\). Equivalently, the monetary boundary is \(250(49.5)=12375\), aggregate mean is 10000, and standard deviation is \(250\sqrt{40}\). A half monetary unit would be the wrong correction.

Exercise 5. A large expected count without a central limit. For Nn taking 0 or 2n equally often and severity 1, compute standardized aggregates and compare their probability at zero with the normal.

Mean and standard deviation are both \(n\). The standardized variable takes −1 and 1 with equal probabilities. Its cumulative probability at zero is 1/2, matching normal there, but at 1/2 it remains 1/2 whereas \(\Phi(1/2)>1/2\). Matching one threshold does not establish distributional convergence. The changing count law fails the concentration structure used by the compound Poisson limit.

16.9 An exact benchmark before an approximation

The original program compares exact gamma survival with normal tails for several fixed counts, and checks compound-Poisson moment scaling. It exposes both approximation error and the cost of using the wrong aggregate variance. All examples are synthetic and explicitly specified.

"""Original exact gamma tails compared with fixed-count normal tails."""
from math import erfc, exp, isclose, sqrt


def gamma_integer_tail(shape, x):
    term = exp(-x)
    total = term
    for k in range(1, shape):
        term *= x / k
        total += term
    return total


for count in (2, 10, 50):
    threshold = count + sqrt(count)
    exact = gamma_integer_tail(count, threshold)
    normal = erfc(1 / sqrt(2)) / 2
    assert 0 < exact < 1
    print(f"n={count:2d}, threshold={threshold:.6f}, "
          f"exact={exact:.8f}, normal={normal:.8f}")
assert isclose(gamma_integer_tail(2, 3), 4 * exp(-3))
fixed_variance = 100 * 9
compound_variance = 100 * (9 + 5**2)
assert compound_variance - fixed_variance == 2500
print(f"Fixed-count variance: {fixed_variance}")
print(f"Poisson-count variance: {compound_variance}")
