A probability and insurance laboratory

Vary a model parameter, predict the consequence, then compare the exact formula. These original interactive models use synthetic inputs and stated assumptions.

From an exponential loss to a compound-Poisson payment

Assume ground-up losses are independent exponential variables with mean \(\theta>0\). A policy has ordinary deductible \(d\ge0\), payment cap \(u>0\), and no coinsurance. A Poisson count \(N\) with mean \(\lambda\) counts ground-up losses and is independent of the severity sequence. Each payment is \(Y=\min((X-d)_+,u)\), including possible zero payments.

\[\mathbb E[Y]=\theta e^{-d/\theta}(1-e^{-u/\theta}),\qquad \mathbb E[Y^2]=2\theta^2e^{-d/\theta}\{1-(1+u/\theta)e^{-u/\theta}\}.\]

These are exact consequences of integrating the exponential survival function. The aggregate mean is \(\lambda\mathbb E[Y]\) and the aggregate variance is \(\lambda\mathbb E[Y^2]\). The count remains the ground-up count while the severity moments include zeros.

Increase the deductible while holding the other inputs fixed: positive-payment probability, mean payment, and aggregate mean all decrease. Increase the cap: the mean and second moment increase toward the uncapped deductible model. An equal change in expected payment need not produce an equal change in variance.

A rare class and an imperfect signal

Let \(H\) denote membership in a specified risk class and \(+\) a positive screening signal. The prior is \(p=\Pr(H)\), sensitivity is \(s=\Pr(+\mid H)\), and false-positive probability is \(f=\Pr(+\mid H^c)\). Total probability and Bayes give \(\Pr(H\mid+)=ps/[ps+(1-p)f]\). This model concerns conditional information; it does not establish causation.

The two joint masses in the denominator come from different population groups. A high sensitivity can coexist with a low posterior when the target class is rare. Changing the prior changes the posterior even if the screening mechanism remains the same.

The diversification limit under common dependence

Assume each of \(n\) losses has variance \(v>0\), and every pair of distinct losses has common correlation \(\rho\in[0,1]\). Expanding the variance of the average gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)/v=1/n+\rho(n-1)/n\). The laboratory shows this ratio. Positive fixed correlation produces a nonzero limiting ratio \(\rho\); adding contracts does not remove that component of risk.

The equal-correlation assumption is an illustrative mathematical model, rather than an estimate for a real portfolio. For \(\rho=0\), the ratio is \(1/n\). For \(\rho=1\), it is one for every portfolio size.
