14.1 A function can encode a distribution

Repeated convolution is a direct route to an aggregate-loss distribution, but it becomes cumbersome for many summands. A transform replaces convolution by multiplication. Its derivatives also recover moments, while its logarithm converts independent addition into ordinary addition. These benefits depend on an existence condition that must be checked before manipulating the function.

For a real random variable \(X\), define its moment-generating function, or MGF, by \(M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]\) at real values \(t\) for which the expectation is finite. The exponential is nonnegative, so an infinite expectation is well defined, but is not a finite MGF value. Always \(M_X(0)=1\). In the standard MGF theory used here, we require \(M_X(t)<\infty\) throughout some open interval \((-a,a)\), with \(a>0\).

The parameter \(t\) has reciprocal units to \(X\), making \(tX\) dimensionless. For a monetary loss, changing units changes the horizontal scale of its MGF. A transform is a deterministic function associated with a distribution, not another random payment.

For a finite-valued claim indicator with success probability \(p\), direct expectation gives \(M_X(t)=1-p+pe^t\). At positive \(t\), large losses receive greater exponential weight. That weighting gives MGFs sensitivity to the upper tail, but also causes them to diverge for important heavy-tailed loss models.

14.2 Existence comes before differentiation

If the MGF is finite on a neighborhood of zero, then moments of every positive integer order exist and can be obtained by differentiation:

\[M_X^{(r)}(0)=E[X^r],\qquad r=1,2,\ldots.\]

The interchange between derivative and expectation requires justification because the integrand is unbounded. Choose \(b>0\) such that the MGF is finite at \(-b\) and \(b\). Since \(e^{b|X|}\leq e^{bX}+e^{-bX}\), its expectation is finite. For \(|t|\leq b/2\), the absolute rth derivative of \(e^{tX}\) is bounded by \(|X|^re^{b|X|/2}\). A polynomial grows more slowly than an exponential, so there is a finite constant \(C_r\) with \(u^r\leq C_re^{bu/2}\) for \(u\geq0\). The derivative is consequently bounded by \(C_re^{b|X|}\), an integrable envelope. Dominated differentiation proves the claim.

The first derivative gives the mean. The second gives the second raw moment, so \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=M_X''(0)-[M_X'(0)]^2\). A raw moment is an expectation of a power of \(X\), without centering. Central moments instead use powers of \(X-E[X]\). Confusing the second raw moment with variance adds an erroneous mean-square term to a risk calculation.

For Bernoulli \(X\), both \(M_X'(0)\) and \(M_X''(0)\) equal \(p\), yielding variance \(p-p^2\). This elementary case exposes the distinction. Differentiation is safe because the MGF is a finite sum of exponentials, finite for every real \(t\).

The converse is false: all moments can exist while no positive MGF value is finite. Let \(Z\) be normal with mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2>0\), and let \(X=e^Z\), a lognormal variable. Completing the square in the normal integral gives \(E[X^r]=E[e^{rZ}]=e^{r\mu+r^2\sigma^2/2}\), finite for every integer \(r\). Yet for \(t>0\), the integrand defining \(M_X(t)\), apart from a positive constant, is

\[\exp\!\left(te^z-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).\]

As \(z\to\infty\), the exponential term \(te^z\) dominates the quadratic term. The exponent is eventually nonnegative and then grows without bound, so the integral over that half-line is infinite. For \(t\leq0\), \(e^{tX}\leq1\), giving a finite expectation. Thus the lognormal has a one-sided Laplace transform but lacks an MGF finite on any open neighborhood of zero. Its moment series cannot be used as an ordinary convergent positive-t MGF.

14.3 Derive the standard transforms

For a Poisson count \(N\) of mean \(\lambda>0\), direct summation gives \(M_N(t)=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{n\geq0}(\lambda e^t)^n/n!=\exp[\lambda(e^t-1)]\), finite for every real \(t\). The equality uses the exponential power series. Differentiating twice gives mean \(\lambda\) and second moment \(\lambda+\lambda^2\), hence variance \(\lambda\).

For a binomial count with \(n\) trials and success probability \(p\), summing the binomial mass gives \(M_N(t)=(1-p+pe^t)^n\) by the binomial theorem. This formula is also the product of \(n\) Bernoulli MGFs, a consequence of independent addition proved below. Its domain is the whole real line because the count is bounded.

An exponential severity with rate \(\beta>0\) has transform \(M_X(t)=\int_0^\infty\beta e^{-(\beta-t)x}dx=\beta/(\beta-t)\) when \(t<\beta\). At \(t=\beta\), the integrand is the constant \(\beta\), so the integral diverges; for larger \(t\), it grows. The domain restriction is part of the answer.

For a gamma severity with shape \(\alpha>0\) and rate \(\beta>0\), the density is \(\beta^\alpha x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}/\Gamma(\alpha)\) for \(x>0\). In the transform integral, replace the rate by \(\beta-t\), then substitute \(u=(\beta-t)x\). The remaining integral is \(\Gamma(\alpha)\), giving \(M_X(t)=[\beta/(\beta-t)]^\alpha\) for \(t<\beta\). This includes exponential severity when \(\alpha=1\), and differentiation gives mean \(\alpha/\beta\) and variance \(\alpha/\beta^2\).

For normal \(X\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2>0\), combine \(tx\) with the normal-density exponent and complete the square:

\[tx-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}=-\frac{(x-\mu-\sigma^2t)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\mu t+\frac{\sigma^2t^2}{2}.\]

The first term integrates to the normalizing integral of a shifted normal density. Thus \(M_X(t)=e^{\mu t+\sigma^2t^2/2}\) for all real \(t\). This derivation also supplies the exponential moments of the normal variable used in the lognormal example. If \(\sigma^2=0\), \(X=\mu\) almost surely and the formula reduces to \(e^{\mu t}\).

A deterministic shift and rescaling satisfy \(M_{aX+b}(t)=E[e^{t(aX+b)}]=e^{bt}M_X(at)\), whenever \(M_X(at)\) is finite. This identity must transport the domain as well as the expression. For example, multiplying an exponential loss by a positive factor \(a\) changes its positive-domain endpoint from \(\beta\) to \(\beta/a\).

14.4 Independent sums multiply transforms

For independent \(X,Y\), \(e^{t(X+Y)}=e^{tX}e^{tY}\), and independence factors the expectation. Thus \(M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)M_Y(t)\) wherever both factors are finite. Repeating gives the product rule for a finite independent family. Dependence prevents the factorization: if \(Y=X\), the correct transform is \(M_X(2t)\), generally different from \(M_X(t)^2\).

Independent Poisson variables with means \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) have sum transform \(\exp[(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)(e^t-1)]\), the Poisson transform with summed mean. Independent gamma variables with shapes \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) and the same rate \(\beta\) have sum transform \([\beta/(\beta-t)]^{\alpha_1+\alpha_2}\), identifying a gamma law with summed shape. Unequal rates do not combine into this same single gamma expression.

Using a transform to identify a distribution invokes a uniqueness theorem: if two probability laws have finite MGFs on a common open neighborhood of zero and the MGFs agree there, then the laws agree. A short justification clarifies why the neighborhood matters. Exponential integrability makes \(E[e^{zX}]\) analytic in a vertical complex strip around the imaginary axis. Equality on a real interval extends throughout the connected common strip by the analytic identity theorem. The transforms therefore agree at \(z=iu\) for every real \(u\), where they are the characteristic functions \(E[e^{iuX}]\). Fourier uniqueness for finite measures then implies equality of the laws.

This argument identifies the precise external analysis results used: analytic continuation and uniqueness of Fourier transforms of probability measures. The elementary aggregate computations do not require reproducing those theorems. Agreement of a few derivatives at zero is much weaker than agreement of MGFs on an interval. A normal and a two-point distribution can share a mean and variance while having entirely different tails.

The normal case gives another exact closure rule. Products of independent normal transforms add the linear coefficients and quadratic coefficients in their exponents. Hence the sum is normal with mean equal to the sum of means and variance equal to the sum of variances. This is an exact finite-sample statement, distinct from a central-limit approximation for nonnormal summands.

14.5 Logarithms isolate additive contributions

When the MGF is finite near zero, it is strictly positive there because its integrand is strictly positive. Define the cumulant-generating function, or CGF, as \(K_X(t)=\log M_X(t)\), and its rth cumulant as \(\kappa_r(X)=K_X^{(r)}(0)\). Since \(M_X(0)=1\), \(K_X(0)=0\). Differentiate to get \(K_X'=M_X'/M_X\) and \(K_X''=M_X''/M_X-(M_X'/M_X)^2\). Therefore the first cumulant is the mean and the second is the variance.

One more derivative gives \(K_X'''(0)=E[X^3]-3E[X^2]E[X]+2E[X]^3=E[(X-E[X])^3]\). The third cumulant is the third central moment. The fourth is \(E[(X-E[X])^4]-3\operatorname{Var}(X)^2\), not the fourth central moment itself. It is obtained by a further differentiation, or by expanding the logarithm of the centered MGF through fourth order. Cumulants beyond the second are not variances and need not be nonnegative.

For independent sums, the product rule becomes \(K_{X+Y}=K_X+K_Y\), so all cumulants add. A shift affects only the first cumulant, while scaling by \(a\) multiplies the rth cumulant by \(a^r\). Both follow from \(K_{aX+b}(t)=bt+K_X(at)\). These rules are useful because central moments above order three do not obey a comparably simple independent-addition rule.

The Poisson CGF is \(\lambda(e^t-1)\); every positive-order cumulant equals \(\lambda\). The gamma CGF is \(-\alpha\log(1-t/\beta)\); differentiation gives \(\kappa_r=\alpha(r-1)!/\beta^r\). The normal CGF is the quadratic \(\mu t+\sigma^2t^2/2\), so its cumulants above order two vanish. In particular, independent normal sums retain a quadratic CGF.

For an original gamma severity with shape three and rate two, the first four cumulants are \(3/2,3/4,3/4,9/8\). Standardized skewness, the third central moment divided by \(\sigma^3\), is \(2/\sqrt3\); excess kurtosis, the fourth central moment divided by \(\sigma^4\) minus three, is two. The factorial and rate powers in the cumulants distinguish these dimensionless shape measures from raw moments.

14.6 A random sum composes transforms

Let \(N\) be a nonnegative integer random variable. Let \(Y_1,Y_2,\ldots\) be iid severities independent of \(N\), and set \(S=\sum_{i=1}^NY_i\), with empty sum zero. Conditional on \(N=n\), independence gives \(E[e^{tS}\mid N=n]=M_Y(t)^n\). Averaging over the count yields

\[M_S(t)=E[M_Y(t)^N]=M_N(\log M_Y(t)).\]

This expression requires a finite, positive \(M_Y(t)\) and a finite value of \(M_N\) at \(\log M_Y(t)\). A sufficient local condition is that the severity and count MGFs are both finite on neighborhoods of zero. Continuity of \(M_Y\) at zero then puts \(\log M_Y(t)\) inside the count's domain for all sufficiently small \(|t|\). Having many finite moments alone does not justify this composition near positive \(t\).

The same result can be written \(G_N(M_Y(t))\), where \(G_N(z)=E[z^N]\) is the probability-generating function for \(z\geq0\) where finite. The argument of the count MGF is a logarithm because MGFs use \(e^{tN}\); the argument of the probability-generating function is the severity MGF itself. Substituting \(M_N(M_Y(t))\) would confuse the two transforms.

For a Poisson count of mean \(\lambda\), the aggregate CGF is \(K_S(t)=\lambda(M_Y(t)-1)\). Differentiating r times at zero gives \(\kappa_r(S)=\lambda E[Y^r]\) whenever the local MGF assumptions hold. In particular, the mean is \(\lambda E[Y]\), and the variance is \(\lambda E[Y^2]\). The second expression contains the severity second raw moment, rather than its variance, because random claim count contributes additional variation.

For a general count, \(K_S(t)=K_N(\log M_Y(t))=K_N(K_Y(t))\). By the chain rule, \(K_S'(0)=E[N]E[Y]\) and \(K_S''(0)=\operatorname{Var}(N)E[Y]^2+E[N]\operatorname{Var}(Y)\). This recovers the conditional-moment derivation of the random-sum formulas. Two different methods reaching the same result expose which term comes from count variation.

Take \(\lambda=2\) and severity equally likely one or three. Then \(M_Y(t)=(e^t+e^{3t})/2\) and \(K_S(t)=e^t+e^{3t}-2\). The first four cumulants are \(4,10,28,82\). The variance ten matches \(E[N]\operatorname{Var}(Y)+\operatorname{Var}(N)E[Y]^2=2(1)+2(4)\). At zero, \(M_S(0)=1\), as every valid transform must satisfy.

14.7 Exponential bounds and model limits

Poisson thinning is a complete distributional consequence of the compound formula. Let \(N\) be Poisson with mean \(\lambda\), and independently mark each event with probability \(p\), with independent marks across events. Represent each mark by a Bernoulli severity \(Y_i\), and let \(B=\sum_{i=1}^NY_i\) count marked events. Substituting \(M_Y(t)=1-p+pe^t\) gives \(M_B(t)=\exp[\lambda p(e^t-1)]\), finite for every real \(t\). MGF uniqueness identifies \(B\) as Poisson with mean \(\lambda p\). This proves the full law, not only its mean and variance.

For positive continuous severities and Poisson count, the aggregate is generally mixed rather than purely continuous: \(P(S=0)=e^{-\lambda}\). An MGF can identify this law despite the absence of a single density representing its entire probability. More generally, uniqueness identifies a probability distribution; it does not promise that the distribution has a density or belongs to a named elementary family. Direct inversion, when required, must retain discrete masses as well as continuous components.

A finite positive MGF value also supplies an upper-tail bound. For \(t>0\), the event \(X\geq x\) implies \(e^{tX}\geq e^{tx}\). The elementary inequality \(E[W]\geq aP(W\geq a)\) for nonnegative \(W\) gives \(P(X\geq x)\leq e^{-tx}M_X(t)\). Minimizing over admissible positive \(t\) gives a Chernoff bound. It is a bound under the model, not an exact tail probability.

For Poisson \(N\) of mean \(\lambda\) and threshold \(x>\lambda\), the exponent is \(-tx+\lambda(e^t-1)\). Its derivative vanishes at \(t=\log(x/\lambda)>0\), giving the bound \(\exp[-x\log(x/\lambda)+x-\lambda]\). Convexity of the exponent, whose second derivative is \(\lambda e^t>0\), confirms the minimum. The bound generally exceeds the actual tail probability because the inequality discards information.

The lognormal example has no admissible positive \(t\), so this particular method gives no useful positive-exponential bound. Other tools remain available: direct integration, simulation with error analysis, inequalities using finite moments, or Laplace and characteristic transforms used appropriately. A method's failure does not imply a distribution is invalid.

The fourth-cumulant expression can be derived explicitly from the centered transform. Let \(W=X-E[X]\), let \(v=E[W^2]\), \(m_3=E[W^3]\), and \(m_4=E[W^4]\). The local MGF assumption justifies its Taylor expansion, which begins \(M_W(t)=1+vt^2/2+m_3t^3/6+m_4t^4/24+O(t^5)\). Use \(\log(1+u)=u-u^2/2+O(u^3)\), with \(u=O(t^2)\). Through order four the only quadratic correction is \(-v^2t^4/8\). Therefore the coefficient of \(t^4\) in \(K_W\) is \((m_4-3v^2)/24\), proving \(\kappa_4=m_4-3v^2\). Since a fourth derivative multiplies the coefficient by \(4!=24\), no factorial remains in the cumulant itself.

This calculation also explains why independent-sum fourth central moments contain cross terms. If independent centered variables \(W_1,W_2\) have variances \(v_1,v_2\), cumulant addition gives \(m_{4,S}-3(v_1+v_2)^2=(m_{4,1}-3v_1^2)+(m_{4,2}-3v_2^2)\). Rearranging yields \(m_{4,S}=m_{4,1}+m_{4,2}+6v_1v_2\). Direct expansion of \((W_1+W_2)^4\) gives the same coefficient six; terms with a single centered factor vanish by independence. Cumulants organize this algebra without pretending that raw or central moments all add.

Convexity provides another diagnostic for a proposed CGF. Fix an interior point \(t\) of the finite MGF domain and define a tilted probability law by weighting the original law with \(e^{tX}/M_X(t)\). This weight is positive and has expectation one, so it indeed defines a probability law. Under this tilted law, write expectation as \(E_t\). Differentiation gives \(K_X'(t)=E_t[X]\) and \(K_X''(t)=E_t[X^2]-E_t[X]^2=\operatorname{Var}_t(X)\geq0\). Thus the CGF is convex wherever these derivatives are justified, not merely at zero.

For a nonconstant variable, exponential tilting preserves which events have zero probability, because the weight is strictly positive. Its tilted variance is consequently positive, making the CGF strictly convex on the interior of its domain. This observation explains the uniqueness of an interior minimizing parameter in many Chernoff calculations. Differentiating \(K_X(t)-tx\) sets \(K_X'(t)=x\), meaning that the tilted mean equals the threshold being bounded. It is an exact identity about a changed probability law, not an assertion that the original loss mean equals the tail threshold.

For a Poisson variable, the tilted mass at \(n\) is proportional to \(e^{tn}e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n/n!\). After normalization it is Poisson with mean \(\lambda e^t\). Setting that mean equal to \(x\) recovers \(t=\log(x/\lambda)\), exactly the optimizer derived above. The computation gives a second interpretation of the exponential tail bound and a useful starting point for importance-sampling methods, although an error-controlled simulation algorithm requires additional work beyond this identity.

Mixtures transform by averaging, rather than multiplying. If a finite class label \(Z\) selects component laws with weights \(w_j\), conditioning gives \(M_X(t)=\sum_jw_jM_{X\mid Z=j}(t)\) wherever the sum is finite. The CGF is the logarithm of this weighted sum, not the weighted sum of component CGFs. A mixture of deterministic payments zero and two with equal weights has MGF \((1+e^{2t})/2\), whereas adding those two deterministic payments has MGF \(e^{2t}\). The selection-versus-addition distinction remains visible after transformation.

The transform framework now handles exact independent sums, random sums, moments, and exponential bounds. Each calculation begins with the domain, then uses independence or conditioning explicitly. This sequence prevents a formal-looking expression from being mistaken for a finite transform of the stated model. It also connects the previous chapter's mixture and compound constructions: mixture averages conditional transforms, independent addition multiplies them, and a random count composes them.

14.8 Exercises with worked solutions

It is useful to distinguish an MGF from a formal moment series. If the MGF is finite near zero, Taylor expansion gives \(M_X(t)=\sum_{r\geq0}E[X^r]t^r/r!\) for sufficiently small \(|t|\). Exponential integrability justifies the exchange of summation and expectation in a smaller neighborhood. In its absence, writing the same sequence of coefficients produces a formal expression that need not converge at any positive argument. The lognormal moments illustrate this sharply: the ratio between successive positive terms is \(t\exp[\mu+(r+1/2)\sigma^2]/(r+1)\), which tends to infinity for every fixed \(t>0\). The series therefore diverges, consistently with the integral argument.

There is a practical dimensional check as well. The rth raw moment and rth cumulant both have units equal to the rth power of the payment unit. In a compound Poisson model, \(\lambda E[Y^2]\) has squared-payment units, as a variance must. Substituting \(\lambda E[Y]\) for that variance would have the wrong units before any numerical comparison. A count is dimensionless, but a rate parameter tied to a time horizon must be converted to the contract horizon before using it as the mean count.

For each exercise, give the domain alongside the transform, then identify which theorem or conditional calculation connects it to the requested quantity. If an aggregate includes zero claims, its transform still starts at one when \(t=0\), because the empty-sum convention contributes \(e^0=1\). These checks catch domain errors, missing count terms, and confusion between MGFs and probability-generating functions.

Exercise 1. Derive the MGF and variance of a payment equal to 0 or 5 with probabilities 0.9 and 0.1.

The MGF is \(0.9+0.1e^{5t}\), finite for every real \(t\). Its first two derivatives at zero are \(0.5\) and \(2.5\). Variance is \(2.5-0.25=2.25\). The nonunit payment size changes the rth raw moment by a factor \(5^r\).

Exercise 2. Independent gamma variables have shapes 2 and 5 and common rate 3. Identify their sum and its first three cumulants.

The MGF product is \((3/(3-t))^7\), finite for \(t<3\), so uniqueness identifies gamma shape seven and rate three. The cumulants are \(7/3\), \(7/9\), and \(14/27\). The first is the mean, second variance, and third the third central moment.

Exercise 3. Explain why a lognormal severity's finite first four moments do not justify an MGF-based Chernoff bound.

The bound needs at least one finite positive \(M_Y(t)\). For nondegenerate lognormal \(Y=e^Z\), \(te^z\) eventually dominates the quadratic normal-density exponent for every \(t>0\), so the defining integral diverges. Finitely many moments, or even all moments, do not supply positive exponential integrability.

Exercise 4. Poisson count has mean 3 and independent exponential severity has rate 2. Derive the aggregate CGF, its domain, mean, variance, and third cumulant.

The severity MGF is \(2/(2-t)\) for \(t<2\), so \(K_S(t)=3(2/(2-t)-1)\) on that domain. Severity raw moments are \(r!/2^r\). Hence aggregate cumulants are \(3/2\), \(3/2\), and \(9/4\) for orders one, two, and three. At \(t\geq2\), the event \(N=1\) already has positive probability and infinite conditional exponential moment, proving divergence.

Exercise 5. Let N be binomial(n,p) and severities be independent exponential(rate β), independent of N. Obtain the aggregate MGF and variance.

The count probability-generating function is \((1-p+pz)^n\). Therefore \(M_S(t)=[1-p+p\beta/(\beta-t)]^n\) for \(t<\beta\), assuming \(n\geq1\) and \(p>0\). Conditioning gives variance \(np/\beta^2+np(1-p)/\beta^2=np(2-p)/\beta^2\). If \(p=0\) or \(n=0\), the aggregate is zero and its MGF is one for all real \(t\).

Exercise 6. For Poisson mean 2, compute the exponential bound for P(N≥6), and compare its expression with the exact tail.

The optimizing parameter is \(\log3\). The bound is \(\exp(4-6\log3)=e^4/729\). The exact tail is \(1-e^{-2}\sum_{k=0}^5 2^k/k!\). These are different quantities; the program below evaluates both and checks that the bound is above the exact probability.

14.9 Check transform identities and a tail bound

The program computes a compound Poisson transform by a truncated count sum and compares it with the closed form. It separately evaluates the exact Poisson tail and its Chernoff bound. Truncation tolerance controls this finite numerical calculation; the algebraic identities above supply the general results.

"""Check compound transforms, cumulants, and a Poisson tail bound."""
from math import exp, factorial, isclose, log


rate = 2
t = .1
severity_mgf = (exp(t)+exp(3*t))/2
closed = exp(rate*(severity_mgf-1))
count_sum = sum(exp(-rate)*rate**n/factorial(n)*severity_mgf**n
                for n in range(60))
assert isclose(count_sum, closed, rel_tol=1e-12)
cumulants = [rate*(1+3**r)/2 for r in range(1, 5)]
assert cumulants == [4, 10, 28, 82]
tail = 1-exp(-rate)*sum(rate**k/factorial(k) for k in range(6))
bound = exp(-6*log(6/rate)+6-rate)
assert 0 < tail < bound < 1
assert isclose(exp(rate*((1+1)/2-1)), 1)
print(f"compound MGF at 0.1 = {closed:.10f}")
print(f"count-sum MGF at 0.1 = {count_sum:.10f}")
print("compound cumulants = " + ", ".join(f"{x:g}" for x in cumulants))
print(f"Poisson P(N >= 6) = {tail:.10f}")
print(f"Chernoff bound = {bound:.10f}")

compound MGF at 0.1 = 1.5762202365
count-sum MGF at 0.1 = 1.5762202365
compound cumulants = 4, 10, 28, 82
Poisson P(N >= 6) = 0.0165636085
Chernoff bound = 0.0748945817
